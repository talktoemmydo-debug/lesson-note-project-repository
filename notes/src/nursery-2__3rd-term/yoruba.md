# Yoruba — Nursery 2 · Third Term

Weeks 1 to 6, 8, 9 and 11. No note for Week 7 (Mid-Term Break) or Week 10 (Revision);
**Week 11 carries a note** because the school's own table prints it as a teaching row
("Exam Preparation"), not as an examination week. Week 12 is Closing.
*Depth: this subject is school-generated — there is no NERDC Yoruba scheme to floor it against. The
extent of each week is the three strands the school itself prints (Ẹ̀dẹ̀ · Àṣà · Lítíréṣọ̀). The master
corpus prints Yoruba without tone marks; tone marks are added in the body of the note so that the
child reads the word correctly, and the topic line is left exactly as the school prints it. Where the
school names a song or a poem by its first words only, the note keeps that one line and teaches the
form, the answer and the meaning — it does not invent the rest.*

### WEEK 1 — Ede: Sise nnkan (ase oluko) / Asa: Itesiwaju ikini ni orisirisi akoko / Litireso: Orin ere idaraya

*Depth: school-generated — three strands as printed in the master corpus for N2 T3 W1.*

**Main content — Ẹ̀dẹ̀ (ṣíe nǹkan — the actions the teacher names)**

• A word that tells us to do a thing is a **command**: **ẹ dú!** (stand), **ẹ jó!** (sit down), **ẹ rìn!** (walk).
• More commands we use in class every day: **ẹ ka** (read), **ẹ kọ́** (write), **ẹ wo** (look), **ẹ gbọ́** (listen), **ẹ dáhùn** (reply).
• To be polite we put **jọ̀wọ́** in the middle: **"Ẹ jọ̀wọ́, ẹ dú."** — stand, please.
• Saying **ẹ** before the word is how we speak to a teacher or to many people; **má ṣe** stops a thing: **"Ẹ má ṣe sọ̀rọ̀."**
• Each action has its own body: **fọ́** — wash, **ṣí** — open, **tì** — shut, **fi sí** — put on, **yọ** — take off.
• **Ẹ wò ó, ẹ sì tún un ka** — look at the line on the board and then read it again.
• Game: an action is called out and we do it; when **"Dúró!"** (stop!) is called, nobody moves.
• We answer in a whole line, not one word: **"Mo ń kà á."** (I am reading it), **"Mo ń kọ́ ọ́."** (I am writing it).
• New words go in the notebook with the action drawn small beside each — a hand, a book, a foot.

**Main content — Àṣà (ìtẹ̀síwájú ìkíni — greetings at their times)**

• Morning: **"Ẹ káàárọ̀."** — the answer is **"Ẹ ṣe àárọ̀ rere."**
• Afternoon: **"Ẹ ká ọ̀sán."** — the answer is **"Ẹ ṣe ọ̀sán rere."**
• Evening: **"Ẹ káàlẹ́."** — the elder answers **"Ọ́ àáṣẹ̀."**
• To ask how people are: **"Báwo ni?"** — the answer is **"Àà báwo ni."** (it is well).
• For a thing done for us we say **"Ẹ ṣé."**; the answer is **"À ń ṣé."**
• A boy greets an elder by lying full length (**ìdòlẹ̀kọ̀**); a girl kneels (**ìkúnbálẹ̀**); both rise when they are told.
• We greet the first person we meet in the morning, and we greet all of them in turn — nobody is skipped.
• Time changes, the greeting changes; the respect stays the same.

**Main content — Lítíréṣọ̀ (orin eré ìdárayá — the play song)**

• A play song is not sung sitting down: it carries the step, the clap and the turn.
• The leader sings a line; the class **answers the same line back** — that is how a play song is built.
• Our rule this week: **while the song goes on we move; when the song stops, we stand still.**
• We sing in a ring, and the child in the middle picks the next line.
• The song teaches the ears: a child who can answer the line can answer the teacher too.

**Words for my notebook**

Ẹ dú · ẹ jó · ẹ ka · ẹ kọ́ · ẹ wo · ẹ gbọ́. Ẹ káàárọ̀ · ẹ ká ọ̀sán · ẹ káàlẹ́ · ọ́ àáṣẹ̀ · báwo ni — àà báwo ni · ẹ ṣé.

**Let us talk**

1. What do I say when I want the whole class to sit down?
2. Add the polite word to "ẹ dú".
3. Which greeting belongs to the evening, and what does the elder answer?
4. How does a girl greet an elder, and how does a boy greet?

**Worksheet**

1. "Ẹ jó!" tells the class to ____ . A) stand B) sit down C) read D) wash
2. The polite word we put inside a command is ____ . A) ṣé B) jọ̀wọ́ C) àáṣẹ̀ D) báwo
3. "Ẹ ká ọ̀sán" is the greeting for ____ . A) afternoon B) morning C) night D) Monday
4. When the elder is greeted in the evening, he answers ____ . A) ẹ ṣé B) báwo ni C) ọ́ àáṣẹ̀ D) àà báwo ni
5. Fill in: The answer to "Báwo ni?" is "Àà ____ ni."
6. Say four actions the teacher names in Yoruba, and do them.
7. Sing one line of the play song and answer it back as the class does.

**My own work**

Write the six actions in your notebook and draw a small hand, book or foot beside each; then colour the one
you like best.

### WEEK 2 — Ede: Siso oro onileta po (F+K+F) / Asa: Orisirisi eso ile wa / Litireso: Alo apamo ati alo apagbe

*Depth: school-generated — three strands as printed in the master corpus for N2 T3 W2.*

**Main content — Ẹ̀dẹ̀ (sọ̀rọ̀ onílétà pọ̀ — joining the letters)**

• A Yoruba syllable is short and clean: **à**, **kà**, **rà** — one vowel with one consonant, and the tone rides on it.
• The school writes the drill **fawẹ̀lì + kọ́nsonantì + fawẹ̀lì**: a vowel, a consonant, a vowel — **ọ + kọ́ = ọkọ́**.
• Say it slowly first, then at once: **à** (pause) **kà** (pause) **rà** (pause) — **àkàrà**, bread.
• More words we join: **bà** + **tà = bàtà** (shoes) · **ò** + **gò** + **gò = ògò́gò** (a pot) · **ẹ̀** + **kẹ́ = ẹ̀kẹ́** (beads).
• The dots change the word: **ọ** is not **o**, **ẹ** is not **e**; **àkàrà** without its dots is not the word.
• Tone is part of the letter: **ọkọ́** (husband) and **òkọ̀** (hoe) are written with the same shapes and say two things.
• We count the sounds on our fingers: **à-kà-rá** is three sounds, three fingers up.
• Every day this week, one new word is joined on the board before we read it.

**Main content — Àṣà (oríṣiríṣi ẹsọ́ ilé wa — the fruits of our land)**

• **ọ̀gẹ̀dẹ̀** — banana · **áńbà** — mango · **ọ̀pẹ̀** — palm fruit · **bílíbilí** — watermelon · **àmùtì** — pineapple.
• Ọ̀gẹ̀dẹ̀ and áńbà come from a tree in the compound; ọ̀pẹ̀ comes from the palm and bílíbilí from the ground.
• A fruit is ripe when it gives us its own sign: the banana turns yellow, the mango softens, the watermelon knocks hollow.
• The palm fruit is boiled and pressed and it becomes the red oil our soup is made with.
• When a child picks a fruit from a neighbour's tree, he asks first; a Yoruba home shares what it has and gives the first of the season to an elder.
• Fruits wash before they are eaten, and the peel goes into the bin, not on the path.

**Main content — Lítíréṣọ̀ (àlọ́ àpamọ́ àti àlọ́ àpàgbé)**

• An àlọ́ begins with a call and an answer: **"Àlọ́ o!"** — **"Àpamọ́!"**
• **Àlọ́ àpamọ́** hides a lesson inside it; when it ends, the hearer can say what he learnt.
• **Àlọ́ àpàgbé** is the light one: it is told to make us laugh and to carry the evening away (gbàgbé is to forget).
• At an àlọ́ we sit, we listen, and we do not finish another person's story with a lie.
• The teller may stop in the middle and ask the children a question; the one who answers goes on with the story.

**Words for my notebook**

à-kà-rá = àkàrà · bà-tà = bàtà · ò-gò-gò = ògò́gò · ọ-kọ́ = ọkọ́. Ọ̀gẹ̀dẹ̀, áńbà, ọ̀pẹ̀, bílíbilí, àmùtì.
Àlọ́ o — Àpamọ́!

**Let us talk**

1. Join the three sounds of à-kà-rá and say the word.
2. Which two fruits in our list come from a tree?
3. What do we say to answer "Àlọ́ o!"?
4. Tell me one thing an àlọ́ àpamọ́ does that àlọ́ àpàgbé does not.

**Worksheet**

1. "à-kà-rá" joined together says ____ . A) kàrà B) àkàrà C) ráà D) àrákà
2. The word "bàtà" is made of ____ sounds. A) one B) three C) four D) two
3. "ọ̀pẹ̀" is the ____ fruit. A) mango B) banana C) palm D) watermelon
4. When somebody says "Àlọ́ o!", we answer ____ . A) Àpamọ́! B) Ẹ ṣé! C) Báwo ni! D) Àà ṣé!
5. Fill in: The dots under **ẹ** and **ọ** change the ____ of the word.
6. Name five fruits of our land and say where one of them comes from.
7. Say two lines about an àlọ́ you have heard and what it taught.

**My own work**

Write five joined words, and under each one draw the thing it names; colour the fruit you would like to eat.

### WEEK 3 — Ede: Isorongbesi laarin oluko ati akekoo / Asa: Imototo: ara fifo, enu fifo / Litireso: Orin kiko

*Depth: school-generated — three strands as printed in the master corpus for N2 T3 W3.*

**Main content — Ẹ̀dẹ̀ (íṣọ̀rọ̀ńbẹ̀sí — teacher and pupil talking)**

• The pupil opens with respect: **"Báwo ni, olùkọ́?"** — the teacher: **"Ó dára, ọmọ mi."**
• To ask for a thing: **"Ẹ jọ̀wọ́, fi ìwé sí mi."** — to be given: **"Ẹ ṣé, olùkọ́."**
• When a question is asked and you know, say **"Mi ò, mi ò"** (me, me) with the hand up, not a shout.
• Answer in a line, not one word: **"Kí ni ẹ̀ ní?"** — **"Mo ní àkọ̀wé àti pencil."**
• When you did not hear, say **"Ẹ jọ̀wọ́, tún un sọ"** (say it again) — that is not rudeness.
• Asking about a thing: **"Ṣé èyí jẹ́ ti mi?"** (is this mine?) · Answer: **"Bẹ́ẹ̀ni"** (yes) or **"Rárá"** (no).
• At the end of the talk both say **"À ń ṣé."**
• When **"Ẹ sí i"** (go on) is said, the next child speaks and the class listens to the end.

**Main content — Àṣà (ìmọ́tótó: ara fọ́, ẹnu fọ́)**

• The body is washed twice a day — **òwúrọ̀** before school and **ìṣẹ́** (evening) before rest.
• Hands wash with soap before food and after the toilet; the nails are cut short, not bitten.
• The mouth is washed in the morning and at night; that is when **ẹnu fọ́** is done, with the brush or the twig.
• Cloth for school is clean and pressed; a cloth worn yesterday for play is not today's cloth.
• Feet wash and dry before the sandals go on; a wet shoe spoils and the foot itches.
• Food is covered, the compound is swept, and the water in the bucket is for washing, not for drinking.

**Main content — Lítíréṣọ̀ (orin kíkó — the carrying song)**

• A carrying song is sung when hands are busy: it keeps the step of the load and the step of the feet together.
• One person **leads the line**, everybody else **carries the answer**: "ọ̀wọ́ ọ̀wọ́ — ẹẹ!"
• The song does not hurry the back; it gives the back a rhythm to rest on.
• When the load comes down, the song ends with the last line — it does not just stop.

**Words for my notebook**

Báwo ni, olùkọ́? — Ó dára, ọmọ mi. Ẹ jọ̀wọ́, tún un sọ. Bẹ́ẹ̀ni · rárá · À ń ṣé. Ara ń fọ́ òwúrọ̀ àti ìṣẹ́; ẹnu ń fọ́ ní méjèèjì.

**Let us talk**

1. How do you greet your teacher in the morning?
2. What do you say when a word was too fast for you?
3. At what two times is the body washed?
4. Who leads a carrying song, and who answers?

**Worksheet**

1. "Báwo ni, olùkọ́?" is said by ____ . A) the pupil B) the guard C) a baby D) the cook
2. When we did not hear a word, we say ____ . A) ẹ ṣé B) rárá C) tún un sọ D) mi ò
3. "Bẹ́ẹ̀ni" means ____ . A) maybe B) no C) please D) yes
4. The mouth is washed ____ a day. A) twice B) never C) once in a month D) only on Friday
5. Fill in: Before food and after the toilet, the ____ wash with soap.
6. Say four things a clean child does every day.
7. Sing one line of a carrying song and let the class answer it.

**My own work**

Draw a cup, a brush and a bar of soap in three boxes on one page, and write one Yoruba word under each box.

### WEEK 4 — Ede: Onka 21-40 (aropo ati ayokuro) / Asa: Ojo inu ose (itan ojo kookan) / Litireso: Ewi kika

*Depth: school-generated — three strands as printed in the master corpus for N2 T3 W4.*

**Main content — Ẹ̀dẹ̀ (oníka 21–40 — àròpọ̀ àti àyọkúrò)**

• Twenty is **ogún**; from there we keep adding with **àti**: **ogún àti ọ̀kan** (21), **ogún àti èjì** (22), **ogún àti ẹ̀ta** (23).
• Thirty has its own name: **ọ̀rúnmọ́lẹ́dógún** — and it is ogún àti ẹ́wàá (20 + 10).
• Forty is **ogótà**; fifty is **ọ̀ọ́dúnrún** — the two big steps we say without counting up.
• Àròpọ̀ (adding): **ogún àti ọ̀kan** plus **ọ̀kan** = **ogún àti èjì** (21 + 1 = 22).
• Àyọkúrò (taking away): **ogún àti ẹ̀ta** less **ọ̀kan** = **ogún àti èjì** (23 − 1 = 22).
• We count with the fingers and with the pebbles: ten pebbles make one line on the board.
• Say the ladder out loud: 21, 22, 23 … 40; then come back down from 40 to 21 without falling.
• The marks of the number are written small in the notebook: **ogún àti ẹ̀rin = 24**.

**Main content — Àṣà (ọjọ́ inú ọ̀ṣẹ́ — the story of each day)**

• **Ọjọ́ Àìkú** — Sunday: the day of thanks and rest; "àìkú" is not-dying, so we sing and we visit.
• **Ọjọ́ Ayé** — Monday: the day the world opens; market, farm and school all begin again.
• **Ọjọ́ Ìṣẹ́gun** — Tuesday: the day of victory; the work that began on Monday is pushed through.
• **Ọjọ́rú** — Wednesday: the day in the middle; the week is half gone and we do not drop.
• **Ọjọ́bọ̀** — Thursday: the day of the cow and the big market; things are bought for the weekend.
• **Ọjọ́ Ẹtì** — Friday: the last edge of the working week; Muslims worship on this day.
• **Ọjọ́ Àbámẹ́ta** — Saturday: the day we meet again — compound swept, hair combed, play allowed.
• Seven days make one ọ̀ṣẹ́; the week has no day we sleep through.

**Main content — Lítíréṣọ̀ (èwì kíkà — reading a poem out)**

• A poem is not read like a tale: it has room inside it for the breath.
• One line, one breath; a comma is a stop, a full stop is a rest.
• The voice goes **up** at a question and **down** at the end of a line.
• We read the line once slowly, once at the right speed, and once from memory.
• A hard word is not skipped: the class says it alone, then puts it back in the line.

**Words for my notebook**

Ogún àti ọ̀kan (21) · ogún àti èjì (22) · ogún àti ẹ̀ta (23) · ọ̀rúnmọ́lẹ́dógún (30) · ogótà (40).
Ọjọ́ Àìkú, Ayé, Ìṣẹ́gun, Ọjọ́rú, Ọjọ́bọ̀, Ẹtì, Àbámẹ́ta.

**Let us talk**

1. Say 21, 22 and 23 in Yoruba.
2. Which day is ọ̀rúnmọ́lẹ́dógún built from two numbers?
3. Name the day the market is big and the day of thanks.
4. What do we do with a hard word in a poem?

**Worksheet**

1. "Ogún àti ẹ̀ta" is the number ____ . A) 23 B) 30 C) 13 D) 32
2. Forty in Yoruba is ____ . A) ọ̀ọ́dúnrún B) ogótà C) ogún D) ẹ́wàá
3. The first day of the week, the day of thanks, is ____ . A) Ọjọ́bọ̀ B) Ọjọ́ Ayé C) Ọjọ́ Àìkú D) Ọjọ́rú
4. Ọjọ́ Àbámẹ́ta is ____ . A) Wednesday B) Friday C) Sunday D) Saturday
5. Fill in: Ogún àti ọ̀kan plus ọ̀kan is ogún àti ____ .
6. Write the seven days of the week in order.
7. Read one line of this week's poem twice: once slowly, once at the right speed.

**My own work**

Write 21 to 30 in figures and in Yoruba on one folded sheet, then cut the sheet into ten cards and shuffle them for the class to read.

### WEEK 5 — Ede: Awon awo olorisirisi / Asa: Iwa rere ati iwa ti ko to / Litireso: Ewi: Ise ni oogun ise

*Depth: school-generated — three strands as printed in the master corpus for N2 T3 W5.*

**Main content — Ẹ̀dẹ̀ (àwọ̀ olóríṣiríṣi — the many colours)**

• **funfun** — white · **dúdú** — black · **púpà** — red · **àwọ̀ ọ̀sàn** — orange · **àwọ̀ ewé** — green · **àwọ̀ òfurufú** — blue.
• The colour of the leaf is **àwọ̀ ewé** because it is the colour of ẹwẹ̀; the colour of the sky is **àwọ̀ òfurufú**.
• **àwọ̀ ilẹ̀** is brown, the colour of the road after rain, and **àwọ̀ omi** is the pale colour of water in a calabash.
• Ask and answer: **"Kí ni àwọ̀ bọ́ọ̀lù yìí?"** — **"Púpà ni."** (it is red).
• A uniform has its colour, a cap has its own; we say both words: **"àwọ̀ ọ̀sàn àti funfun."**
• Things may share a colour and not a name: the milk and the paper are both **funfun**.
• **àwọ̀ wúnì** is purple, and **fàdákà** is the pale shine of a new coin.
• The colour comes after the thing: **bọ́ọ̀lù púpà** (a red ball), **ìwé funfun** (a white book).

**Main content — Àṣà (ìwà rere àti ìwà tí kò dára)**

• **Ìwà rere** is good character: **ọ̀wọ̀** (respect), **ìrẹ̀lẹ̀** (humility), **òtítọ́** (truth), **ìpín** (sharing), **kíkó nǹkan ṣe ní títò** (doing a thing well).
• **Ìwà tí kò dára** is the bad kind: **èké** (a lie), **ìjìgbe** (taking what is not ours), **ìpaniyaníyàn** (making a child afraid), **àìmọ́tótó** (dirtiness) and **sísọ̀rọ̀ àyò** (tale-bearing).
• An **ọmọlúàbí** is the child the whole street trusts: he greets, he tells the truth, he leaves a place better than he found it.
• A good act done once is a start; done every day, it becomes ìwà — the character the elders name.
• When we do wrong, we say **"Ì jẹ́ mi, ẹ̀ṣẹ́ mi ni"** (it is my fault) and put the thing right.

**Main content — Lítíréṣọ̀ (èwì: ìṣẹ́ ni òògùn ìṣẹ́)**

• The line the school prints is: **"Iṣẹ́ ni òògùn ìẹ́, tí kò bá ṣiṣẹ́, òògùn wo ló ń lò ó?"**
• It says: work is the medicine for shame; a child who will not work has no medicine left.
• The first half answers the question, and the second half asks it back — that is how èwì moves.
• We say it with the hand on the chest, once slowly and twice at speed, and then we say what it means.

**Words for my notebook**

Funfun · dúdú · púpà · àwọ̀ ọ̀sàn · àwọ̀ ewé · àwọ̀ òfurufú · àwọ̀ ilẹ̀. Ìwà rere: ọ̀wọ̀, òtítọ́, ìrẹ̀lẹ̀, ìpín. Iṣẹ́ ni òògùn ìẹ́.

**Let us talk**

1. What colour is the sky in Yoruba?
2. Name three things of the colour funfun.
3. Which word is the opposite of òtítọ́?
4. What does the poem say is the medicine for shame?

**Worksheet**

1. The Yoruba for red is ____ . A) funfun B) dúdú C) púpà D) àwọ̀ ewé
2. "àwọ̀ ewé" is the colour of ____ . A) a leaf B) the sky C) milk D) a stone
3. Which of these is ìwà rere? A) èké B) òtítọ́ C) ìjìgbe D) ìpaniyaníyàn
4. "Iṣẹ́ ni òògùn ìẹ́" teaches the value of ____ . A) sleeping B) play C) work D) wealth
5. Fill in: A child the whole street trusts is called ọmọ____ .
6. Name six colours in Yoruba and say the colour of your school bag.
7. Say the poem line and write what it means in one English sentence.

**My own work**

Colour one leaf green, one ball red, one cup white and one sky blue, and write the Yoruba colour word on each.

### WEEK 6 — Ede: Apeko (gbo, ko sile) / Asa: Ojuse ebi: egbon ati aburo / Litireso: Orin iwa (Kini n o fi ole se)

*Depth: school-generated — three strands as printed in the master corpus for N2 T3 W6.*

**Main content — Ẹ̀dẹ̀ (Apeko: gbọ́, kọ́ sílẹ̀ — hear it, write it)**

• Sit straight, hold the pencil, and keep both hands on the book — that is how the ear opens.
• The word is said **twice**: the first time we hear it, the second time we catch its marks.
• We write the dots and the tones as we hear them: **ẹ̀kẹ́**, not "eke".
• Nobody looks at a friend's book; a wrong word written is a word we will fix together after.
• When all have written, the class reads the line back and the board is checked.
• Words this week: **àkàrà · bàtà · ìwé · ọkọ́ · ògò́gò · ẹ̀kẹ́** — six words, six sounds.
• A wrong word is not rubbed hard: cross it with one clean line and write it again.
• The last thing is reading our own line back; a missing mark is added before the book goes up.

**Main content — Àṣà (ojúṣe ẹbí: ẹ̀gbọ́n àti àbúrò)**

• **Ẹ̀gbọ́n** is the elder sibling; his duty is to lead by the hand, to share what he has, and to be the example.
• An ẹ̀gbọ́n does not raise his hand on a smaller child; he carries the load and calls the younger one to wash.
• **Àbúrò** is the younger; her duty is to listen, to follow, and to ask before she takes.
• A younger child does not hide an elder's book, and an elder does not eat the younger's share.
• When the parents are away, the ẹ̀gbọ́n keeps the house quiet and the àbúrò keeps the compound clean.
• The two are one hand: one cannot comb the other's hair alone, and both greet the same elder at the same time.

**Main content — Lítíréṣọ̀ (orin ìwà: "Kí ni n ń fi ọ̀lẹ̀ ṣe?")**

• The song the school prints is called by its question: **"Kí ni n ń fi ọ̀lẹ̀ ṣe?"** — what do we do with a thief?
• A character song asks the class something and waits: the answer is the song's other half.
• Our answers this week: we do not beat him, we ask him why, we take the thing back, and we tell a grown-up.
• Sing the question; answer it in a line; the class that answers loudest sings the next question.

### WEEK 8 — Ede: Akosoro (ka, pe iro, fa koko yo) / Asa: Oge sise ni ile Yoruba / Litireso: Orin kiko (Ori ewe ma paya)

*Depth: school-generated — three strands as printed in the master corpus for N2 T3 W8.*

**Main content — Ẹ̀dẹ̀ (akọ̀sọ̀rọ̀: kà, sọ ọ́ yarayara, fa kọ́kọ́ yọ)**

• An **akọ̀sọ̀rọ̀** is a line that is hard on the mouth; we practise it until the mouth is free.
• Step one: **kà á pẹ́lẹ́** — read it slowly, one word at a time, with the eyes on the board.
• Step two: **sọ ọ́ yarayara** — say it fast three times, and do not bend the tones.
• **Fa kọ́kọ́ yọ** is to pull out the knot: find the one word that trips you and say only that word ten times.
• Our lines this week: **"Ẹyin ẹyẹ kan, ẹyin ẹyẹ méjì."** · **"Ọ̀wọ́ọ̀n mẹ́ta, ẹran mẹ́ta."** · **"Àkàrà mẹ́fà, ọ̀gẹ̀dẹ̀ mẹ́rin."**
• Nobody laughs at a mouth that is learning; we clap the one who tries again.
• In **ẹyin ẹyẹ** the first two sounds are alike, and the mouth must not slide them into one.
• A hard name is said by the whole class slowly once before anybody says it fast.

**Main content — Àṣà (ọ̀gẹ̀ ṣíṣe ní ilé Yorùbá — cassava work at home)**

• **Ọ̀gẹ̀** is cassava, and the work of it is the work of the whole house.
• **A ń wa ọ̀gẹ̀** — we dig it out of the ground, and the long root is cut from the stem.
• **A bo ọ̀gẹ̀, a fọ́ ọ** — it is peeled and washed until the white inside shows.
• **A ge é gege** — it is grated, put into a bag, and **a fún un** — pressed, so that the water goes out.
• The wet flour is **ṣíṣẹ́** (sieved) and then **ìfọ̀** (fried) in a dry pot; that is **gari**.
• From the same ọ̀gẹ̀ we get **ẹ̀kọ́**, **àmàlà** from the dried chips, and **ẹ̀bà** from gari — nothing of it is thrown away.
• The work needs the big hands of a grown-up at the fire and at the press; a child carries the basin and counts the cups.

**Main content — Lítíréṣọ̀ (orin kíkó: "Ori ewe ma paya")**

• The school prints this carrying song by its first line only — **Orí ẹwẹ̀, má ṣe payà** — so we learn that line and its answer.
• The leader carries the line; the class carries the answer after him, the way a load is shared by two.
• The song keeps the feet: one step to one word, and the load does not shake.
• When we do not know the rest of a song, we do not stop — we sing the line we know twice.

**Words for my notebook**

Ẹyin ẹyẹ kan, ẹyin ẹyẹ méjì. Ọ̀gẹ̀: a wa á, a bo ó, a fọ́ ọ, a ge é gege, a fún un, a ṣe gari. Orí ẹwẹ̀, má ṣe payà.

**Let us talk**

1. What are the two speeds of an akọ̀sọ̀rọ̀?
2. Say the cassava work in order, from the ground to the pot.
3. Who leads a carrying song and who answers?
4. What do we do when we do not know the rest of a song?

**Worksheet**

1. "Fa kọ́kọ́ yọ" means ____ . A) eat the food B) sweep the yard C) pull out the knot D) fetch the water
2. The first thing done to ọ̀gẹ̀ in the house is ____ . A) frying B) peeling and washing C) selling D) drying in rain
3. Gari is made by frying the ____ flour. A) sieved B) burnt C) frozen D) sweetened
4. A carrying song is answered by ____ . A) the dog B) one child alone C) the class after the leader D) the drum only
5. Fill in: Before the pot goes on the fire, the wet flour is pressed so that the ____ goes out.
6. Say five steps of cassava work in order.
7. Say one akọ̀sọ̀rọ̀ line slowly, then fast, and write what was hard for your mouth.

**My own work**

Draw five small boxes and put one cassava step in each box — dug, peeled, washed, grated, fried — then number the boxes 1 to 5.

### WEEK 9 — Ede: Didaruko eya ara / Asa: Oba alade ni ile Yoruba / Litireso: Aroso lori eye

*Depth: school-generated — three strands as printed in the master corpus for N2 T3 W9.*

**Main content — Ẹ̀dẹ̀ (dídárúkọ̀ ẹ̀yà ara — the parts of the body)**

• **orí** — head · **ojú** — eye · **etí** — ear · **imú** — nose · **ẹnu** — mouth · **ahọ́n** — tongue.
• **ọwọ́** — hand · **ìka** — finger · **ẹsẹ̀** — leg · **àṣọ̀ ẹsẹ̀** — the shoe · **ọ̀rùn** — neck · **ẹ̀yìn** — back · **ikùn** — stomach.
• The teeth are **ehín**, the hair is **ìrìn**, and the ear's inside keeps the sound — **eti** hears.
• Game: **"Ẹ fi ọwọ́ kan ..."** (put the hand on ...) — orí, ọ̀rùn, ikùn, ẹsẹ̀ — fast, and nobody looks at a friend.
• Two eyes see, two ears hear, two hands work; one mouth, so we speak less than we hear.
• A body part that hurts is **told** the same day: "Orí mi ń ṣà àìlẹ̀" (my head pains me).
• **ejín** is the knee and **ìkún ọwọ́** is the elbow; the two of them bend so that we can sit and reach.
• Most of them come in pairs — ojú méjì, etí méjì, ọwọ́ méjì — and one orí, ẹnu kan.

**Main content — Àṣà (Òba aláde ní ilẹ̀ Yorùbá — the crowned king)**

• The **Òba** is the ruler of a Yoruba town, and **àdé** is the beaded crown he wears.
• The crown's beads hang before his face, so that the king is seen by all and looks on all with patience.
• Under the Òba are his **chiefs**, and over a village is the **Baale**.
• Great titles among the Yoruba: **Ọọni** (Ìlé-Ifẹ̀), **Aláàfin** (Ọ̀yọ́), **Àwújàlẹ̀** (Ìjẹ̀bú), **Olúbadan** (Ìbàdàn).
• A man greets the Òba by lying down (**ìdòlẹ̀kọ̀**), a woman by kneeling (**ìkúnbálẹ̀**); the words are **"Kábíyèsí o."**
• The Òba does not run a market or settle a small quarrel; he keeps the town together and is the father of it.
• A child's part is small: when the procession passes, we stand, we do not shout, and we follow with the eyes.

**Main content — Lítíréṣọ̀ (àròsọ lórí ẹyẹ — a riddle line on a bird)**

• An **àròsọ** is a short line said fast, with a thing hidden in it; the class guesses.
• It begins with a call — **"Àròsọ o!"** — and the answer is **"Jẹ́ kó o!"** (let it come).
• Our line about a bird goes by what the bird does, not by its name: **"Ẹyẹ tí ń fò, tí kò ṣẹ̀ṣẹ̀ wọ ilẹ̀ — ẹnu ńlá, ẹsẹ̀ kékeré."**
• Then we say what the riddle means, and the class makes up a line of its own about a bird they know.

**Words for my notebook**

Orí · ojú · etí · imú · ẹnu · ahọ́n · ọwọ́ · ìka · ẹsẹ̀ · ọ̀rùn · ẹ̀yìn · ikùn. Òba ń ṣàdé; a ń kí i: Kábíyèsí o.

**Let us talk**

1. Point to three parts of the body and say them in Yoruba.
2. What is the crown of the Òba called, and what hangs before his face?
3. How does a woman greet the Òba?
4. What do we say to answer "Àròsọ o!"?

**Worksheet**

1. "Ahọ́n" is the ____ . A) ear B) nose C) tongue D) neck
2. The beaded crown of the Òba is the ____ . A) àdé B) ìka C) ẹ̀kẹ́ D) asọ̀
3. The head of a village, under the Òba, is the ____ . A) Ọọni B) Olówọ́ C) Aláàfin D) Baale
4. "Ẹ fi ọwọ́ kan ẹsẹ̀" tells you to touch the ____ . A) foot B) back C) head D) hand
5. Fill in: The riddle call "Àròsọ o!" is answered "Jẹ́ kó o!" — a short line with a thing ____ in it.
6. Name eight parts of the body in Yoruba.
7. Make one àròsọ line about a bird and write what it means.

**My own work**

Draw a boy from the top of the head to the feet, and write the Yoruba name beside the head, the hand, the foot and the mouth.
