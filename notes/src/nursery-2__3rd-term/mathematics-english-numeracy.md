### WEEK 1 — HALVES AND QUARTERS

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Mathematics · Third Term W1, held down to the Nursery floor.*

**You will learn to**

• share two things equally between two friends
• say what half of a thing is
• find half of a shape and shade it
• fold a shape into four equal parts and say the name of one part

**Things to know**

• **Equal** means the parts are the same size; two parts of different sizes are **not** halves.
• When one orange is shared between two children so that both get the same, each one has **half** — written
  **½**.
• A **half** is one of **two equal parts** of a thing; the whole thing is two halves put back together.
• A circle folded once and cut on the fold gives two halves; a square folded corner to corner gives two
  halves too — they are triangles.
• To **shade half** of a shape, divide it into two equal parts and colour one of them — one part only.
• A rectangle can be halved down the middle or across the middle; both are right, because the two parts match.
• Fold a paper circle twice, once and then again, and you have **four equal parts**: each part is a
  **quarter**, written **¼**.
• Four quarters make the whole; two quarters make a half; so a quarter is smaller than a half.
• Sharing game: eight counters between two children is **four each** — that is half of eight; eight between
  four children is **two each** — that is a quarter of eight.

**Words for my notebook**

Half is one of two equal parts, and it is written ½. Quarter is one of four equal parts, and it is written ¼.
Two halves make the whole, and four quarters make the whole.

**Let us talk**

1. What must the two parts be like before we can call them halves?
2. Show me how to fold a paper circle into two halves.
3. How many quarters make one whole?
4. If six biscuits are shared among two children, how many does each get?

![A cake with one piece cut out, a circle cut into two equal halves, and a circle cut into four equal quarters.](assets/img/nursery-2/3rd-term/mathematics-english-numeracy/week1-shapes-cut-in-halves-and-quarters.png)
**Worksheet**

1. When a thing is cut into two equal parts, each part is a ____ . A) quarter B) whole C) half D) side
2. The mark **½** stands for ____ . A) one half B) two halves C) one quarter D) four wholes
3. Which of these is NOT a half? ____ . A) two equal parts B) a fold that matches C) two quarters joined D) the bigger unequal piece
4. A paper circle folded twice and cut gives ____ . A) two halves B) four quarters C) three parts D) one whole
5. Fill in: Four ____ make one whole.
6. Draw a square and shade half of it; then say how you divided it.
7. Write two sentences on sharing ten shells between two friends.

**My own work**

Fold a paper square into two equal parts and cut along the fold; then draw a circle, a square and a rectangle
and shade half of each one.

### WEEK 2 — LENGTH: HOW LONG IS IT?

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · Numeracy · Third Term W7 and Primary 1 · Mathematics · Third Term W2.*

**You will learn to**

• say which of two things is longer or shorter
• measure with the body: the hand span and the foot
• measure with a pencil, a stick or a piece of thread
• put three things in order from the shortest to the longest

**Things to know**

• **Length** is how long a thing is from one end to the other.
• We compare before we measure: this strap is **longer**, that one is **shorter**, and two may be **the same
  length**.
• A **hand span** — thumb out to little finger out — is a body measure; so is the **foot**, the **arm** from
  elbow to fingertip, and one **step**.
• To measure with a hand span, we put the spans side by side with **no gap and no overlap**, and count them.
• A **pencil**, a **stick** or a piece of **thread** can carry a measure across, and then we count how many
  pencils the desk is.
• The same table measured with a hand and with a foot gives **different numbers**, because the units are not
  the same size — so people agree on one unit.
• The small unit for length is the **centimetre (cm)** and the big one is the **metre (m)**; one metre is a
  long step, and a ruler is marked in centimetres.
• On a ruler the little **lines** are the centimetres; we lay the thing on the line that begins at **zero**,
  not at the edge of the ruler.
• Order three things by length and say it out: "The stick is the longest, the crayon is the shortest, and the
  pencil is between them."
• Estimating is a skill: first say how many spans you think it will be, then measure and see how near you were.

**Words for my notebook**

Length is how long a thing is from end to end. We can measure with a hand span, a foot, a pencil or a stick,
and we can measure with a ruler in centimetres; one metre is 100 centimetres.

**Let us talk**

1. Which is longer, your exercise book or your pencil purse?
2. How many hand spans is your desk?
3. Why do we count the spans with no gap between them?
4. What do the little lines on a ruler stand for?


![A boy spans the desk with both hands and marks three spans along the top; on the floor a crayon, a pencil and a stick lie from shortest to longest.](assets/img/nursery-2/3rd-term/mathematics-english-numeracy/week2-the-hand-span-desk.png)
**Worksheet**

1. Length of a thing is how ____ it is from end to end. A) long B) heavy C) hot D) loud
2. Measuring a desk with your hand span uses a ____ measure. A) paper B) water C) money D) body
3. One metre is the same as ____ centimetres. A) ten B) one hundred C) fifty D) five hundred
4. When two ropes are the same length, ____ . A) one is longer B) both are short C) they match end to end D) one is heavier
5. Fill in: We begin to measure on the line marked ____, not on the edge of the ruler.
6. Put three things in your class in order from shortest to longest and write the order.
7. Write two sentences on what you measured with your foot and how many steps it was.

**My own work**

Measure three things on your table with your hand span and write the number of spans beside each; then draw a
crayon, a pencil and a stick in a row from the shortest to the longest.

### WEEK 3 — WEIGHT: HEAVY AND LIGHT

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 2 · Numeracy · Third Term W8, with Primary 1 · Mathematics · Third Term W3.*

**You will learn to**

• say which of two things is heavier or lighter
• sort things into heavy, middle and light
• compare weights with a hanger balance
• know that a big thing is not always the heavier thing

**Things to know**

• **Weight** is how heavy a thing is — how hard your hand has to work to lift it.
• We say **heavier**, **lighter**, **the heaviest**, **the lightest**, and **the same weight**.
• Lifting two things in two hands is the first way to tell: the hand that goes down found the heavier thing.
• A **hanger balance** — a coat hanger with two cups on string — shows it without lifting: the heavier side
  goes down.
• When the two cups stand level, the two things are **the same weight**.
• We can weigh with **blocks**: how many blocks does it take to bring the cup level? That number is the
  weight in blocks.
• **Big is not always heavy**: a big balloon is lighter than a small stone; a bunch of cotton wool is bigger
  than a chalk and still lighter.
• In the market things are weighed: fruits, yam, rice and oil; the seller puts them on a scale.
• The small unit of weight is the **gram (g)** and the big one is the **kilogram (kg)**; a small stone is some
  grams, and a bag of rice is counted in kilograms.
• Weighing in real life: your school bag on Monday and on Friday may not be the same weight, because the books
  inside are different.
• Thinking question: if an orange is heavier than a grape, which one is lighter? — the grape.

**Words for my notebook**

Weight is how heavy a thing is. The heavier side goes down on a balance, and when the cups are level the two
things weigh the same. A big thing is not always heavier: a balloon is bigger than a stone and lighter.

**Let us talk**

1. Which is heavier, your chair or your bag?
2. What does a level balance tell you about the two cups?
3. Name one big thing that is light and one small thing that is heavy.
4. How many blocks does your pencil purse need to make the cups level?


![A hanger balance with two cups: one stone pulls its cup down, three blocks in the other cup sit higher, and a girl points at the difference.](assets/img/nursery-2/3rd-term/mathematics-english-numeracy/week3-the-hanger-balance.png)
**Worksheet**

1. Weight tells us how ____ a thing is. A) long B) tall C) wide D) heavy
2. On a balance, the heavier side ____ . A) stays up B) goes down C) swings away D) disappears
3. A big balloon and a small stone: which is lighter? ____ . A) the stone B) neither C) the balloon D) both
4. To weigh with blocks, we count how many blocks make the cups ____ . A) level B) empty C) wet D) higher
5. Fill in: The big unit of weight is the kilo____ .
6. Sort six things in your class into heavy, middle and light and write the three lists.
7. Write two sentences on a thing you lifted that surprised you.

**My own work**

Draw a hanger balance with a stone in one cup and three blocks in the other, and let the stone side hang
lower; then colour the blocks.

### WEEK 4 — TIME: THE DAY, THE WEEK AND THE CLOCK

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Mathematics · Third Term W4, with Primary 2 · Mathematics · Second Term W9–W10.*

**You will learn to**

• say the parts of the day and the days of the week in order
• use yesterday, today and tomorrow rightly
• name the parts of a clock and read the hour on it
• say what o'clock and half past look like on the clock face

**Things to know**

• The day has parts: **morning**, **midday**, **afternoon**, **evening** and **night**.
• The week has **seven days**: Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, Saturday, Sunday.
• **Yesterday** is the day that is gone, **today** is the day we are in, **tomorrow** is the day that is coming.
• A clock is drawn as a **face** with the numbers **1 to 12** round it and **two hands** in the middle.
• The **short hand** counts the hours and the **long hand** counts the minutes; the short hand moves slowly,
  the long hand moves fast.
• It is **o'clock** when the long hand stands straight up at 12; where the short hand points is the hour — so
  long hand at 12 and short hand at 9 is **9 o'clock**.
• It is **half past** when the long hand points down at 6 and the short hand stands past a number: long hand
  at 6, short hand past 3, is **half past 3**.
• A quarter of the way round is **quarter past**; three quarters is **quarter to** the hour that is coming.
• **a.m.** is the morning side of noon and **p.m.** is from noon to night; school hours are a.m. and supper
  time is p.m.
• Things take time: sweeping the yard is short, a farm day is long; we say "it took the morning", "it took two
  hours".
• Our day has an order — wake, wash, eat, school, learn, play, bathe, eat, sleep — and the clock tells us
  when each part comes.

**Words for my notebook**

The week has seven days and the day has its parts. The short hand counts the hours and the long hand counts
the minutes; when the long hand is at 12 it is o'clock, and when it is at 6 it is half past.

**Let us talk**

1. Say the seven days, beginning from Monday.
2. What do we call the day that comes after today?
3. Where is the long hand when it is o'clock?
4. Point to the morning part of the day on the clock and say what we do then.


![A clock face with twelve marks and no numerals: the short hand points left, the long hand points up — nine o'clock — with a bag, a cup and a broom below.](assets/img/nursery-2/3rd-term/mathematics-english-numeracy/week4-nine-oclock.png)
**Worksheet**

1. The day that has already gone is ____ . A) today B) yesterday C) tomorrow D) midday
2. How many days make a week? ____ . A) five B) ten C) seven D) twelve
3. At o'clock, the long hand stands at ____ . A) 12 B) 6 C) 3 D) 9
4. The short hand on a clock tells the ____ . A) minutes only B) days C) months D) hours
5. Fill in: When the long hand points down at 6, it is half ____.
6. Write the parts of the day in order, from the time you wake to the time you sleep.
7. Write two sentences on how long it takes you to come to school and what you pass on the way.

**My own work**

Draw a big clock face with twelve short marks round the edge — or the numbers 1 to 12 — and put the short
hand on 9 with the long hand straight up. Then draw three things you do at that hour in the morning.

### WEEK 5 — SHAPES YOU CAN HOLD: THREE-DIMENSIONAL SHAPES

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Mathematics · Third Term W7.*

**You will learn to**

• name five shapes that take up room
• count the flat faces, the edges and the corners of a box
• sort shapes into the ones that roll and the ones that stack
• find these shapes in the house and the class

**Things to know**

• A shape you can **hold** takes up room; we call it a **three-dimensional** shape.
• A **cube** has six flat faces and every face is a square; a die and a small carton of sugar are cubes.
• A **cuboid** also has six flat faces, but its faces are rectangles; a exercise book, a matchbox and a
  carton of milk are cuboids.
• A **sphere** is round all over, has no flat face and no corner, and it rolls any way you put it; a ball is a
  sphere.
• A **cylinder** has two round flat faces — one at the top and one at the bottom — and a curved side; a tin of
  milk, a cup and a bucket are cylinders.
• A **cone** has one round flat face at the bottom, a curved side and **one corner** at the top; a party cap
  and a heap-sieve are cones.
• A **face** is a flat surface, an **edge** is where two faces meet, and a **corner** is where edges meet.
• A cube has **12 edges** and **8 corners**; a cuboid has the same, because it is a stretched cube.
• Rolling test: the sphere and the cylinder roll; the cube and the cuboid do not roll, they slide — and those
  two can **stack** without falling.
• Building: cubes make a wall that stands; balls make a heap that runs away; a cylinder laid on its side
  becomes a roller.
• Look round the class: the clock on the wall is a cylinder seen from the front, the ball in the store is a
  sphere, the chalk box is a cuboid.

**Words for my notebook**

A cube has six square faces; a cuboid has six rectangular faces; a sphere rolls and has no flat face; a
cylinder has two round faces; a cone has one round face and one corner. Faces, edges and corners are what we
count.

**Let us talk**

1. Bring one thing in the class that is a cuboid and one that is a sphere.
2. How many flat faces has a cube?
3. Which two of our five shapes will roll?
4. Where is the one corner of a cone?

![Five things you can hold on a shelf: a cube, a ball, a round tin, a party cap and a long box.](assets/img/nursery-2/3rd-term/mathematics-english-numeracy/week5-five-things-you-can-hold.png)
**Worksheet**

1. A ball is shaped like a ____ . A) sphere B) cube C) cone D) cuboid
2. How many flat faces has a cube? ____ . A) four B) eight C) ten D) six
3. Which shape has one round face and one corner? ____ . A) cylinder B) cone C) cube D) sphere
4. A matchbox and an exercise book are both ____ . A) spheres B) cones C) cuboids D) cylinders
5. Fill in: The place where two faces meet is called an ____.
6. Sort ten things at home into the five shapes and write two of each.
7. Write two sentences on the shape you can stack and the shape you cannot.

**My own work**

Set a cube, a ball, a tin and a cap on the desk, look at them, then draw all four in a row and colour the tin.

### WEEK 6 — FLAT SHAPES: TWO-DIMENSIONAL SHAPES

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Mathematics · Third Term W8.*

**You will learn to**

• name five flat shapes
• count the sides and the corners of each
• draw a square, a rectangle and a triangle with a ruler
• find flat shapes on the things about us

**Things to know**

• A **flat shape** lies on a surface; it has length and width but nothing to hold.
• A **triangle** has **three sides** and **three corners**; a **square** has four equal sides and four corners.
• A **rectangle** has four sides too, but only the opposite sides are equal — it is a stretched square.
• A **circle** is one curved line with no corner and no side; everything on it is the same distance from the
  middle.
• An **oval** is like a squashed circle: one curved line, no corner — an egg's outline is an oval.
• A **side** is the line between two corners; a **corner** is the point where two sides meet.
• To draw straight, lay the ruler down, draw along it, lift the ruler and turn it at the corner.
• Two triangles put side to side make a rectangle; a square cut from corner to corner makes two triangles.
• Shapes are on everything: the exercise book is a rectangle, the clock's face is a circle, a scarf folded is
  a triangle, a slate is a rectangle.
• Sorting: shapes with corners in one hoop, shapes with no corner in another; four-sided shapes in a third.

**Words for my notebook**

A triangle has three sides and three corners. A square has four equal sides and a rectangle has four sides
with the opposite ones equal. A circle and an oval are curved and have no corner.

**Let us talk**

1. How many corners has a triangle, and how many has a square?
2. Which two flat shapes have no corner at all?
3. Show me how you make a rectangle out of two triangles.
4. Name three things in this room with a rectangular face.


![A house pasted from one square and one triangle with a rectangle door, and beside it a circle, a triangle, a rectangle and an oval waiting to be used.](assets/img/nursery-2/3rd-term/mathematics-english-numeracy/week6-the-shape-house.png)
**Worksheet**

1. A shape with three sides and three corners is a ____ . A) square B) circle C) oval D) triangle
2. How many corners has a rectangle? ____ . A) two B) four C) three D) six
3. Which of these has no corner? ____ . A) a square B) a triangle C) a circle D) a rectangle
4. The sides of a square are ____ . A) equal B) unequal C) curved D) only two
5. Fill in: An oval is like a squashed ____.
6. Draw a triangle, a square and a rectangle with your ruler and count the sides of each.
7. Write two sentences on one thing at home whose face is a circle.

**My own work**

### WEEK 8 — SHAPES THAT GO ON AND ON: PATTERNS AND SYMMETRY

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Mathematics · Third Term W9.*

**You will learn to**

• say the next thing in a pattern of shapes
• make a pattern of your own with shapes and colours
• find the flat shape inside a shape you can hold
• tell a line of symmetry on a folded paper

**Things to know**

• A **pattern** is things repeated in the same order, so that we can say what comes next.
• In **square, circle, square, circle**, the part that repeats is two shapes; the next is **square**.
• In **triangle, triangle, circle, triangle, triangle, circle**, the part that repeats is three shapes.
• A pattern can grow: one block, then two, then three — the next line has **four** blocks.
• Colour takes part in a pattern too: red, blue, red, blue is as much a pattern as shape is.
• Take a cube and look at one face: it is a **square**; a **cylinder** has a **circle** at the top; a cone
  looks like a **triangle** from the side.
• A flat shape can be made from other shapes: two triangles make a square; four triangles make a bigger
  triangle; a square and two triangles make a house.
• **Symmetry** means the same on both sides: fold a paper heart and the two halves match all the way round.
• The fold line where the two halves match is the **line of symmetry**; a square can be folded to match in four
  ways, a rectangle in two.
• A circle has no corner to miss: every fold through the middle matches.
• Art with shapes: cut squares, triangles and circles from paper and make a road, a house and a boat on one
  page.

**Words for my notebook**

A pattern repeats the same order, so the next thing can be said. A cube hides a square on each face and a
cylinder hides a circle at the top. A fold that makes both halves match shows a line of symmetry.

**Let us talk**

1. Square, circle, square, circle — what comes next?
2. What shape do you see on the face of a cube?
3. Show me a fold on this paper heart where both halves match.
4. Which two shapes make one square?


![Square, circle, square, circle, square, circle — and one more square set a little apart to finish the pattern; below it, a folded heart with a dashed middle line.](assets/img/nursery-2/3rd-term/mathematics-english-numeracy/week8-the-shapes-that-repeat.png)
**Worksheet**

1. Circle, square, circle, square — the shape that comes next is a ____ . A) circle B) square C) triangle D) cone
2. Two triangles placed side by side can make ____ . A) a circle B) an oval C) a square D) a cone
3. When a folded paper matches on both sides, the fold line is a line of ____ . A) symmetry B) length C) weight D) count
4. The top of a cylinder is shaped like a ____ . A) triangle B) square C) rectangle D) circle
5. Fill in: In one, two, three, the next number of blocks is ____.
6. Make a pattern of your own with six shapes and write the part that repeats.
7. Write two sentences on one thing you cut and pasted today.

**My own work**

Cut two triangles, one square and one circle from paper, paste them into a picture of a house or a boat, and
draw the line of symmetry on the square part.

### WEEK 9 — COUNTING WHAT THE CLASS SAID: GATHERING INFORMATION

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Mathematics · Third Term W10.*

**You will learn to**

• ask a question and write down the answers
• record counts with strokes that are easy to add up
• put the answers into a simple table
• read a picture chart and a block chart of the class

**Things to know**

• We **gather** a thing by asking and counting, and then we put the answers in order so that the question is answered.
• First the question, small and clear: **"Which fruit do you like best — mango, banana or orange?"**
• Then we go round the class and take one answer from each child, and write it as we hear it.
• A stroke stands for one child; four strokes together and a fifth stroke across them means **five** — that is
  why strokes are easier to count than a long line of single marks.
• Answers are put in a **table**: one column for the fruit, one column for the number of children.
• In a **picture chart** one picture stands for one thing: three orange pictures mean three children chose
  oranges.
• In a **block chart** the tower of blocks stands for the count; the **taller** the tower, the **more** the
  children.
• Reading a chart is answering with the eyes: which tower is the tallest, which is the shortest, and by how
  many one beats another.
• We can gather the same way about colours, about pets, about the way to school, and about the song the class
  likes best.
• The class that counted must **check its total**: the numbers in the table should come to the number of
  children present.

**Words for my notebook**

We ask one clear question, take an answer from each child, and record it with strokes. A table keeps the
answers in order, a picture chart lets one picture stand for one child, and in a block chart the taller tower
means more children.

**Let us talk**

1. What is the first thing we do before we count anything?
2. Why do we put a fifth stroke across four strokes?
3. In a block chart, what does a taller tower tell us?
4. Our table said 8, 7 and 5 for twenty children present — does that add up?


![Three towers of blocks stand on one line, five high, three high and two high, over an orange, a banana and a mango, while a girl holds up a blank slate.](assets/img/nursery-2/3rd-term/mathematics-english-numeracy/week9-three-towers-of-blocks.png)
**Worksheet**

1. Before we count, we first ____ . A) shout in the market B) sleep C) ask one clear question D) draw a clock
2. Four strokes with a fifth stroke across them stand for ____ . A) five B) four C) three D) eight
3. In a picture chart, one picture stands for ____ . A) the class B) a whole week C) ten children D) one child
4. In a block chart, the taller tower means ____ . A) fewer children B) more children C) no child D) a longer day
5. Fill in: The answers are put in a table with one column for the fruit and one for the ____.
6. Ask five friends their favourite colour and record the answers with strokes.
7. Write two sentences on what your class chart showed and what you would ask next.

**My own work**

under the three fruit names and colour the tallest tower.
