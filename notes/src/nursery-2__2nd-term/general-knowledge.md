### WEEK 1 — PEOPLE IN MY COMMUNITY: OUR HELPERS

*Depth: school-generated — the school's own subtopic list for N2 S2 W1.*

**Main content**

• Helpers in my community: **doctor, nurse, teacher, police officer, firefighter, tailor, farmer**, and beside them the **driver, cleaner, water-seller, shopkeeper, mason, barber, welder, midwife**.
• What each one does: the **doctor** treats the sick · the **nurse** gives the medicine and cleans the wound · the **teacher** teaches reading, counting and good manners · the **police officer** keeps us from thieves and stops the road · the **firefighter** puts out fire · the **tailor** sews our cloth · the **farmer** grows the food we eat.
• Where they work: doctor and nurse at the **hospital** or the clinic · teacher at the **school** · police officer at the **police station** or on the road · firefighter at the **fire station** with the red engine · tailor at the **sewing shop** by the market · farmer on the **farm**.
• Why they matter: if the farmer stops, the market is empty; if the nurse stops, the sick have no help; if the police stops, the road is not safe.
• How we say thank you to helpers: greet them, say **"Ẹ̀ ṣé" / "thank you"**, do what they ask, keep the place clean they swept, do not waste what they made, and never spoil their tools.
• Our **community helpers song**: *"Doctor, nurse and teacher too, / police officer and farmer who feeds me — / thank you. Thank you, all of you, / you work for me, I work for you!"* — one line for each helper, with a hand action.
• **Role play**: one child wears the coat, one carries the bag, one holds the ruler; the rest must guess the helper and ask one question — *"What do you do when a child falls?"*
• A **helper who visits the class** is a lesson: the nurse shows how to wash hands, the tailor shows a needle and a tape, the farmer brings two yams and a handful of rice in the pod.
• What I must ask of myself: a helper is not a servant; and one day **I** shall be one of these people.

**Words for my notebook**

Doctor — hospital. Teacher — school. Police — police station. Farmer — farm. We thank helpers by working with them, not by spoiling their things.

**Let us talk**

3. What would happen if the cleaners did not come?
4. How do you thank a helper?

### WEEK 2 — MY COUNTRY — NIGERIA (INTRODUCTION)

*Depth: school-generated — the school's own subtopic list for N2 S2 W2.*

**Main content**

• My country's name is **Nigeria**; my state is **Lagos** (or the state written on my bag), and the country is made of **36 states and the FCT**.
• Saying it out loud: **"My name is ____. I am a Nigerian."** — a name and a country are the two things I carry first.
• The **Nigerian flag** is **green — white — green**, two green bands with a white band in the middle, and it flies at the school gate on Monday.
• What the colours mean (the first idea): **green is for our farms and land** (the trees, the yam, the rice) and **white is for peace** — peace in the middle, holding the two sides together.
• How to hold a flag: it is never dragged, never on the ground, never used as a cloth; it goes up the pole slowly and comes down without flipping.
• **Nigeria on the map**: our country is **big** — it sits in **West Africa**, next to Benin Republic, Niger, Chad and Cameroon. And the sea is at its bottom; from Lagos to the far north is more than a day in a bus.
• Our **capital city is Abuja**; our biggest city, where we are, is **Lagos**.
• Our money is the **Naira**, written **₦**; we use **coins** (₦½, ₦1, ₦2) and **notes** (₦5, ₦10, ₦20, ₦50, ₦100 and above); on the notes are our own faces and our own things.
• Our languages: **English** is the language of the school; **Hausa, Igbo and Yoruba** are the big languages of the people, and there are more than 250 other languages.
• Our **motto** is *"Unity and Faith, Peace and Progress"*; our **anthem** begins *"Arise, O compatriots"*.
• The **song of the week — "One Nigeria"**: *"One Nigeria, one people, / one flag and one song; / thirty-six states, many tongues, / to you, my country, I belong."*
• Colouring the flag: two green sides, one white middle, no other colour; the top line is straight like the gate.

**Words for my notebook**

Nigeria · Abuja · green-white-green · Naira (₦) · "Arise, O compatriots" · I am a Nigerian.

4. Which city is the capital, and which city do we live in?

### WEEK 3 — NIGERIAN FOOD, CLOTHES AND FESTIVALS

*Depth: school-generated — the school's own subtopic list for N2 S2 W3.*

**Main content**

• Foods we grow and eat: **rice, yam, beans, plantain, cassava, maize, groundnut** and the fruits **orange, mango, banana, watermelon, pineapple, agbalumọ̀**.
• Nigerian dishes: **jollof rice, fried rice, coconut rice, pounded yam, eba, amala, tuwo, akara, moi-moi, egg soup, vegetable (ewédú) soup, egusi soup, pepper soup, suya, kosai, pap and bread**.
• Where food begins: the **farm**, then the **market**, then the **cooking pot** — rice comes from a paddy, yam from a mound, garri from cassava pressed and roasted.
• Clothes for every day: **school uniform, T-shirt and shorts, dress and slippers, sandals**; the clothes must be clean and matched, not torn.
• Traditional clothes: **ankara** (the printed cloth), **lace** (the fine see-through cloth), **buba and wrapper**, **iro and gele**, **agbada** (the big flowing robe), **kaftan and trousers** with a **fila** cap, **isiagu** (the lion-head cloth of the east).
• When we wear them: a wedding, a naming ceremony, a festival, church or mosque on a feast day, the last day of term.
• **Festivals we celebrate: Christmas** (the birth of Christ — new cloth, church, food for all), **Eid-el-Fitr and Eid-el-Kabir (Sallah)** (after the fast. And at the rams — meat shared with the neighbour), **New Year**, **birthdays**, and the big cultural days: **Egungun, Osun-Osogbo, Durbar, the New Yam festival, Eyo**.
• At every festival there is **music and dance**: **talking drum (gángan), dùndún, bàtá, udu (clay pot), ogene (metal gong), flute, shaker**; the drum **talks** because the drummer copies the rise and fall of Yoruba words.
• **Sharing food with neighbours** is the law of a festival: the plate that goes next door comes back with food in it; a family that eats alone is a family that has forgotten the festival.
• **Show and tell**: I bring or describe my favourite food, and I come in the cloth of my father's people while the class asks two questions.

**Words for my notebook**

Jollof rice, pounded yam, eba, amala, akara, moi-moi. Ankara, lace, agbada, iro and gele, kaftan. Christmas, Sallah, New Yam, birthdays.

4. Why do we carry food to the neighbour on a feast day?

### WEEK 4 — OUR WEATHER AND SEASONS

*Depth: school-generated — the school's own subtopic list for N2 S2 W4.*

**Main content**

• A day is **sunny** (the sun is out, the sky clear, the ground hot), **cloudy** (the sky is covered, the sun hides), **rainy** (water falls from the cloud), **windy** (the air moves and the trees bend).
• Weather is what the sky is doing **today**; season is the weather that comes **again at the same time each year**.
• Nigeria has two big seasons: the **rainy season** (about **April to October** — water from the sky, farms green, rivers full. Mosquitoes many) and the **dry season** (about **November to March** — no rain, dust, the sun is hard, the harmattan blows).
• The **harmattan** is the dry wind that comes from the **Sahara** in **December to February**: mornings are **cool**, the air is **dusty and hazy**, the sky looks white and thin, lips and hands crack. And the mornings may need a sweater though noon is hot.
• Words for the day: **hot** (harmattan noon, March sun), **warm** (a cloudy day), **cold/cool** (a harmattan morning, rain at night), **humid** (the air feels wet before rain).
• Clothes for each weather: **sun** — hat, light cotton, sandals; **rain** — **umbrella**, rain coat, gum boots, a plastic bag on the bag. **harmattan dust** — a light shawl or sweater, nose and hands washed often; **wind** — nothing loose to carry away.
• Weather tells us what to wear before we dress; the sky tells us whether to bring a bag for books or a bucket for water.
• The **class weather chart**: seven columns (Monday to Sunday), and each day one box is coloured — sun, cloud, rain, wind — with the count of how hot: "họ́t", "cool", "cold" — one word under the picture.
• Our **weather song**: *"Sun in the morning, rain in the night, / harmattan dust and a cool, cool light; / umbrella or sweater, hat or a shawl — / the sky tells me what to wear, after all."*
• What weather can do to us: rain can flood a street, wind can carry a roof, the sun can burn the head. So we do not play in the storm, we do not stand under a lone tree when there is lightning, and we drink water when we work in the sun.

**Words for my notebook**

Sunny · cloudy · rainy · windy. Rainy season (April–October) and dry season (November–March); the harmattan is cool, dusty and hazy.

**Let us talk**

1. What was the sky doing this morning?
2. Which season are we in now, and how can you tell?
3. What clothes do you wear on a rainy morning?
4. What does the harmattan do to your hands and lips?

### WEEK 5 — PLANTS AROUND ME

*Depth: school-generated — the school's own subtopic list for N2 S2 W5.*

**Main content**

• Plants in my environment: **trees** (mango, iroko, oil palm, neem, flame tree), **flowers** (hibiscus, rose), **grass** (the lawn and the roadside), **vegetables** (ugu, waterleaf, spinach, tomatoes, pepper).
• A plant is not only big things: the small green thing at the wall's foot is a plant too, and it has all four parts.
• The four parts to name: **root**, **stem**, **leaf**, **flower** (and then the **fruit** and the **seed**).
• What each part does: the **root** holds the plant in the ground and drinks water from the soil · the **stem** carries water up and holds the plant up · the **leaf** catches sunlight and makes the plant's food · the **flower** becomes the **fruit**, and the fruit keeps the **seed**.
• Where the new plant hides: inside the seed — one seed, one plant; that is why the fruit is a box.
• Fruits I eat: **orange, mango, banana, watermelon, pineapple, agbalumọ̀ (sweetsop), avocado** · Vegetables I eat: **tomato, pepper, ugu, waterleaf, spinach, carrots, onions**.
• What plants give us: **food** (fruit, yam, leaves), **shade** (the tree at the gate), **fresh air** (the leaf takes the bad air and gives the good one we breathe), **medicine** (neem leaf), **wood, cloth, and beauty**.
• Care of a plant: **water it in the cool of the morning**, do not break the young stem, do not burn the grass round it, do not tie a rope to cut the bark. And pull the weeds beside it, not the plant.
• **Planting in class**: a bean seed in a tin of soil, one cup of water, a place with light; we draw the tin on Monday and again a week later.
• What to watch for: first the **soil lifts**, then a **green loop** comes up, then two leaves, then more — and each day the pot is heavier than the day before because the root is going down.
• Plants and weather: in the rainy season everything greens; in the dry season the grass goes yellow and the tree drops its leaves to keep its water.

**Words for my notebook**

Root, stem, leaf, flower, fruit, seed. The root drinks, the stem carries, the leaf makes food, the seed makes a new plant.

### WEEK 6 — MY SENSES AND SIMPLE SAFETY

*Depth: school-generated — the school's own subtopic list for N2 S2 W6.*

**Main content**

• The five **senses** and their **organs**: **sight — eyes** · **hearing — ears** · **smell — nose** · **taste — tongue** · **touch/feel — skin (hands)**.
• What each tells me: the eye tells **colour and shape**; the ear tells **sound and direction**; the nose tells **smell** (a good soup, a gas leak). The tongue tells **taste** — sweet, sour, bitter, salty; the skin tells **hot, cold, rough, smooth, sharp, pain**.
• The senses warn us: a **smell of smoke** tells us to run, a **hot cup** tells the hand to let go, a **bad smell** on milk tells us not to drink it.
• Never test a thing with the wrong sense first: **do not taste or smell an unknown powder; look, then ask** — the nose can be burned by acid fumes.
• Care of the senses: no sharp thing in the ear, no staring at the sun, no loud sound near the ear, eyes kept an arm's length from a screen, hands washed before eating.
• **Safety at home** — the four dangers of a child: **fire** (matches, kerosene stove, burning coal), **hot water** (the pot on the floor. The bath mixed with cold first), **sharp objects** (knife, cutlass, broken glass, razor, nail), **medicines** (a pill that looks like a sweet is not a sweet).
• Home rules: matches are **left where they are**, not picked up; nothing is touched on the cooking table. A broken glass is **reported and swept by a grown-up**, not held in the hand; medicine is taken only when it is given, never from the shelf.
• **Road safety**: hold a grown-up's **hand, not the finger**; stop at the kerb; **look left, right, left again**; **listen** for the engine. Cross at the **bridge (footbridge) or the zebra corner**; never run across and never chase a ball into the road.
• **Stranger danger**: never **follow** a stranger, never **take gifts, sweets or a ride**, never go into a stranger's room or car, shout **"help!"** loudly if somebody holds you. And **tell somebody the same hour** about anything a grown-up asked you to keep secret.
• The **safe and unsafe picture game**: a green card for safe, a red card for unsafe — the hand on the pot is red. The grown-up's hand held is green, the sweet from a stranger is red, the crossing at the bridge is green.
• Safety songs and the class poster: our own poster has four pictures and four rules, and it is put on the door so the whole school sees it.

**Words for my notebook**

Eyes see, ears hear, nose smells, tongue tastes, skin feels. Red card = danger: fire, hot water, sharp things, medicine, strangers.

### WEEK 8 — NIGERIAN ANIMALS, BIRDS AND THEIR HOMES

*Depth: school-generated — the school's own subtopic list for N2 S2 W8.*

**Main content**

• Farm animals and their uses: **goat** (meat and milk, and it eats the weeds round the house), **cow** (milk, meat, and its dung makes the farm ground rich), **chicken** (eggs and meat. And its feathers), **sheep** (wool and meat), **pig** (meat), **rabbit** (meat and fur), and beside them the **duck** (eggs) and the **guinea fowl** (eggs and meat — the common protein of the north).
• What each gives: **eggs** from bird and fowl · **milk** from the cow and the goat · **meat** from nearly all of them · **hide and leather** from the cow and goat · **wool** from the sheep · **manure** from all of them for the farm.
• Animals and their homes: the **dog** lives in a **kennel** · the **chicken** in a **coop (poultry house)** · the **cow** in a **shed/byre** · the **goat** in a **pen** · the **pig** in a **sty** · the **rabbit** in a **hutch** · the **horse** in a **stable** · the **sheep** in a **fold** · and the bush animals make their own: the bird a **nest**, the rat a **burrow**, the python a **hole**, the monkey the **trees**.
• Our **national bird is the eagle** — it is on our coat of arms with the two horses. The eagle **sees far**, so it stands for strength and vision; its house is the top of a tall tree or a rock.
• The **lion** is called the **king of animals** because he walks with his head up and the rest of the bush makes way for him; his sound shakes the bush and his home is the open savannah.
• Other animals of Nigeria to know: the **elephant** (biggest land animal, in the north-east), the **leopard**, the **buffalo**, the **hippo** in the river. The **crocodile**, the **gorilla and chimpanzee** in the Cross River forests, the **python**, the **tortoise** of our stories.
• Animals that live with us in stories: the **tortoise (Ìjàpá)** — clever but greedy · the **rabbit** — quick but easily frightened · the **lion** — strong but to be flattered · and in the tales, the little animal beats the big one with the head, not the leg.
• **Picture matching**: animal to home (dog—kennel), animal to sound (hen—gèé, cock—kòkòrò, goat—mèé, cow—moooo, dog—gbáà! ), animal to baby (cow—calf, goat—kid, sheep—lamb, hen—chick).
• Kindness rule: an animal that lives with us is **fed, watered, kept clean and not tied too short**; a bird's egg taken from the nest for play is a bird that will not come back to our street.

**Words for my notebook**

Dog—kennel · chicken—coop · cow—shed · goat—pen · bird—nest. The eagle is our national bird; the lion is the king of animals.

3. Which bird is on our coat of arms?
4. What does the tortoise teach us in the stories?

### WEEK 9 — SONGS, RHYMES AND STORIES OF NIGERIA

*Depth: school-generated — the school's own subtopic list for N2 S2 W9.*

**Main content**

• A **nursery rhyme** is a small song with a game in it: *"Two two two, eye eye eye"* type action songs, *"Àlọ́, àlọ́! Àlọ́ ni!"* and the counting songs we clap.
• A **lullaby** is the song a mother sings to make a child sleep; it is soft, slow, and it names the child: *"Sleep, my little one; the sun has gone; the bird is in the tree; sleep, sleep, sleep."*
• Nigerian **folk songs** are for work and for play: the **pounding song** (with the pestle), the **water-seller's song**, the **farmer's song**, the **harvest song**, and the dance songs of each people.
• **Folktale 1 — the tortoise and the wisdom of the world**: Ìjàpá put all the world's wisdom in a gourd and went up a tree to hide it, the gourd tied on his front. His small son called out, "Tie it on your back!" — and Ìjàpá knew that a child had more sense than he. He dropped the gourd. It broke, and the wisdom flew out and was scattered for everybody. **Lesson: no person is wise enough alone; wisdom is shared, and a child may teach an elder.**.
• **Why the lizard nods**: the anole (lizard) keeps his head up and down because he once carried a message he forgot on the way — so he nods to ask whether it was right. **Lesson: do what you are sent to do, and finish it.**
• Tales that teach, with their one word: **honesty** (the woodcutter's axe that was not his) · **sharing** (the pot that fed the village) · **obedience** (the girl who did not listen and followed the strange bird) · **hard work** (the two farms — one weeded daily, one left to grass).
• Songs about Nigeria: the **anthem "Arise, O compatriots"** — today only the **first listening**: stand, be quiet, hear the melody once, and say one word you heard (*"compatriots", "toil", "serve", "peace", "justice"*). Our pledge is not yet ours to write, but it is ours to stand for.
• A second Nigeria song for us: *"Nigeria, my country, the land of my birth; / green is the farm and the flag is my worth."* — one verse, with the hand on the chest.
• **Storytelling circle**: one child is the teller, the rest answer *"Àlọ́ ni!"*. The teller shows a picture card, tells the tale in **four lines** — who, what happened, what went wrong, what we learn — then the class **retells** it in their own words, one line each.
• A story that is retold becomes yours; a story that is only heard stays in the class.

**Words for my notebook**

Rhyme, lullaby, folk song, àlọ́. Wisdom is for everybody. "Arise, O compatriots" is our national anthem.
