### WEEK 1 — MARRIAGE

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · SCS · Second Term W3 (pp 657–658).*

**You will learn to**

• say what marriage is
• name the kinds of marriage ceremony we have in Nigeria
• say that marriage brings a family
• name the duties of a husband and a wife

**Things to know**

• **Marriage** is the day a man and a woman are joined to live together as **husband and wife**, before their families and before the law.
• Marriage is the **beginning of a family**: the house, the children and the name all start from it.
• Three kinds of ceremony in Nigeria: **traditional** (family rites, price and blessing by the elders), **religious** (in a **church** or a **mosque**, with vows before God), and **court** (registry — signed papers before a marriage registrar and two witnesses).
• A marriage can have **one or more** of these at once: many couples do the traditional rite, then the church, and then sign at the registry.
• A **bride** is the woman on her wedding day; a **groom** is the man; the **wedding** is the ceremony; the **reception** is the party after it.
• The wedding **attire** carries the culture: **aso-oke, iro and buba, gele, agbada, kaftan, isiagu**; the colour and cloth belong to the family's people.
• In marriage the two **work together**: one does not rule, both decide; they plan the house, the food, the fees and the names.
• The two duties that hold a home: **love** (care shown in words and in work) and **respect** (speaking well of each other, and not before the children).
• A child born of married parents has a **name, a family, a language and a place in the community** — that is what the rite gives.
• Where there is a problem, the wise family **talks**, calls the elders they trust, and does not fight in front of the children.
• In our class we say it plainly: **every child deserves a home**, and a home is kept by two people who keep their promise.

**Words for my notebook**

Marriage joins a man and a woman as husband and wife, and it is how a family begins — by traditional rite, in church or mosque, or at the registry.

4. What two duties hold a home together?

### WEEK 2 — RESPONSIBLE PARENTING

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · SCS · Second Term W4 (pp 657–658).*

**You will learn to**

• say what parenting means
• name what parents must give a child
• tell what makes a good parent
• say what the child owes back

**Things to know**

• **Parenting** is the work of raising a child — feeding, keeping, teaching and loving until the child can stand alone.
• A **parent** is a father, a mother, or the person who stands in their place; **guardian** is the grown-up who keeps a child that is not their own.
• Four responsibilities of parents: **food, clothing, education and love**; without any of the four the child is poor.
• Parents must also give **shelter and health**: a roof, a clean bed, immunization, treatment when there is sickness.
• A good parent is **caring** (notices when something is wrong), **patient** (teaches twice, ten times), **provides** (works so the fridge is not empty) and **teaches right from wrong**.
• A good parent **names the child**, gives the child a **language**, a **faith** or a conscience, and a **duty in the house**.
• Discipline that is responsible: rules that are said before, a correction that is not cruel, and punishment that fits the fault.
• Children's side: **obey**, **respect**, say thank you, help in the house, learn what is taught, and do not lie.
• **Appreciation** is not flattery: it is saying "thank you for the school bag" and doing the work the bag is for.
• A child who is fed but not spoken to has been half-parented; a child who is loved but never corrected is also in trouble.
• Where a child has no father or mother, the **grandmother, an aunt, an older brother or a foster home** does the parenting; the love is the same work.

**Words for my notebook**

Parenting is feeding, clothing, teaching and loving a child. The child answers with obedience, respect and hard work.

**Let us talk**

1. What is parenting?
2. Name the four things a parent must give.
3. What does a good parent do when the child repeats a fault?
4. What does the child give back to the home?

### WEEK 3 — ROLES OF MEMBERS OF THE FAMILY

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · SCS · Second Term W7 (pp 657–658), with NERDC Nursery 2 · Social Habits · First Term W2 (pp 379–382) for the same topic in our own band.*

**You will learn to**

• name the work of each person in my family
• say what a father and a mother do
• say what my own role is as a child
• tell the work of grandparents, uncles and aunts

**Things to know**

• A **family** is the group of people who live together and keep one another: father, mother and children, and often the grandparents.
• **Father's role**: he **provides** (work, money, food, school fees), **protects** the house, **guides and teaches** the children, and **plays with** them.
• A father also decides with the mother — the head of the house is a **team**, not one voice.
• **Mother's role**: she **cares for the children** (feeding, nursing, dressing), **cooks**, **keeps the home in order**, works outside too, and **teaches** manners and language.
• **The children's role**: **obey**, **respect** one another and the elders, **help with the chores of the house**, **study well**, keep the place tidy, do not break what is not yours.
• **Grandparents' role**: they give **advice and wisdom**, **tell the stories** that carry our history, watch the children when the parents work, and teach the culture and the prayers.
• **Extended family**: uncles, aunties and cousins are the **support** of a family — they lend, they keep, they attend the naming ceremony and the funeral.
• Simple chores for a child in Nursery 2: set and clear the plates, sweep one corner, fetch water, arrange the slippers, water a plant, fold my own cloth, keep my books in the bag.
• **Everybody working together makes the home light**: one person sweeping a compound is slow, three persons finish before dusk.
• **Appreciation** is naming what each one does: today the food is cooked, today the fees were paid, today the child washed his own plate — all three are work.
• A role is not a punishment; when the child shares the work of the house, he is being trusted, not used.

**Words for my notebook**

Father provides and protects; mother cares, cooks and teaches; children obey, help and study; grandparents give advice and stories.

**Let us talk**

1. What are two duties of a father?
2. Who cooks and keeps the home in order in your house?
3. Which chore will you take up at home this week?
4. What do grandparents give that school cannot?

**Worksheet**

### WEEK 4 — CULTURE (PARTS 1 AND 2)

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · SCS · Second Term W8 (Culture Part 1) and W9 (Culture Part 2), pp 657–658 — the school keeps both parts in one week.*

**You will learn to**

• say what culture is
• name the elements of culture
• tell the dress, food and greeting of our people
• say how our culture is kept

**Things to know**

• **Culture** is **the way of life of a people**: what they speak, eat, wear, greet, marry, bury, sing and celebrate.
• Culture is learnt, not born with: we catch it from the home, the street and the elders.
• The **elements of culture** to name: **language, food, dressing, festivals, greetings, music and dance, religion, names, marriage and burial rites**.
• Nigeria is **rich and diverse**: three big groups — **Hausa, Igbo, Yoruba** — and over two hundred and fifty other peoples, each with a culture.
• **Traditional dressing**: the **wrapper** and **buba** (loose shirt), the **iro** (wrapper tied round the waist) with **gele** (the head tie), the **kaftan** and the **agbada** (the big flowing robe), the **fila** cap, the **hula/beehive** cap of the north.
• **Nigerian foods** to name: **jollof rice, pounded yam, egusi soup, tuwo, akara, moi-moi, eba, amala, pepper soup, kilishi, suya, kosai**.
• **Greetings** carry respect: the Yoruba boy **prostrates (doba)**, the Yoruba girl **kneels (doble/kunrin)**; the Hausa man puts his hands together and lowers them. The Igbo elder is greeted with "ndewo/nno" and a seat is offered; in the west a handshake with the right hand is common.
• A greeting said well is a culture kept; a child who forgets to greet has begun to forget his people.
• **Festivals** in our community: **Eid-el-Fitr and Eid-el-Kabir** (the Muslim celebrations after the fast and at the rams), **Christmas and Easter**, the **new yam festival (Iwa Ji/Emumu)**, **Egungun, Osun-Osogbo, Durbar of Katsina, Argungu fishing festival, Eyo festival of Lagos**.
• At a festival there is **dance, drum, masquerade, new cloth, special food, horsing, and the blessing of the elders** — that is culture in one place.
• **Preserving culture** means speaking our own language at home, wearing our cloth on our days, learning the folk tales, eating the food of our people, greeting the elders, and writing the names right.
• Culture is not everything old: it is what our people **do with what they have learnt**, so a Nigerian child who codes and greets is not losing his culture.

**Words for my notebook**

Culture is the way of life of a people — language, food, dress, greeting, festival and music. Nigeria has many cultures and we keep ours.

**Let us talk**

1. What is culture?
2. Name the food of our home and the cloth of our father's people.
3. How does a Yoruba girl greet an elder?
4. Name two festivals in Nigeria and what happens at one.

**Worksheet**

1. Culture is best described as A) a market name B) a school subject C) the way of life of a people D) a church rite
2. The cloth wrapped and tied on a Yoruba woman's head is the A) hula B) buba C) fila D) gele
3. Pounded yam, egusi soup and jollof rice belong to Nigerian A) names B) food C) dressing D) music
4. The Yoruba boy's way of greeting an elder is to A) shout B) clap only C) prostrate D) run
5. Fill in: The three big ethnic groups of Nigeria are Hausa, Igbo and ____.
6. Mention five elements of culture.
7. Write two sentences on a festival in our community and one thing done there.

**My own work**

### WEEK 5 — INTRODUCTION TO NIGERIA

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · SCS · Second Term W10 (pp 657–658).*

**You will learn to**

• say that Nigeria is our country and where it is
• name the states and the capital
• name our languages and religions
• say our national motto and know my own state

**Things to know**

• **Nigeria is our country**; it is in **West Africa**, and it faces the sea in the south — the **Atlantic (Bight of Benin)**.
• Nigeria's neighbours: **Benin Republic** on the west, **Niger** on the north, **Chad** on the north-east, **Cameroon** on the east.
• Nigeria has **36 states** and one **Federal Capital Territory (FCT)** — the FCT holds **Abuja**, the **capital city**.
• Before Abuja, the capital was **Lagos**; the seat of the government moved to Abuja in **1991**.
• The largest city and the money town is still **Lagos**; the three big cities are Lagos, Kano and Ibadan.
• Our **official language is English**; it is the language of the school, the court and the paper. Our **national languages** are **Hausa, Igbo and Yoruba**, and there are more than **250 other languages**.
• The major religions are **Christianity, Islam and Traditional religion**; the north is mostly Muslim, the south mostly Christian, and the middle belt holds both and the old faith.
• Nigeria's national motto is **"Unity and Faith, Peace and Progress"** — it is written on the coat of arms.
• The **flag** is **green–white–green**: green for the farms and the land, white for peace.
• Our national anthem is *Arise, O Compatriots*; the pledge is said in school on the flag day.
• **Our own state**: name it, name its capital, and name the **local government** we live in; a citizen knows at least three circles — home town, state, country.
• Nigeria is **the most populous country in Africa** (over 200 million people) and one of the biggest producers of **crude oil** and of **farm produce** — cocoa, groundnut, palm oil, yam and rubber.

**Words for my notebook**

Nigeria is in West Africa: 36 states and the FCT with Abuja as capital. English is our official language; our motto is "Unity and Faith, Peace and Progress".

### WEEK 6 — ATTITUDES AND VALUES

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · SCS · Third Term W1 (pp 659–661).*

**You will learn to**

• say what an attitude is and what a value is
• tell a good attitude from a bad one
• name the core values of Nigeria
• show honesty and hard work in class

**Things to know**

• An **attitude** is how I **think and feel** about a thing, and how that shows in what I do.
• A **value** is what I hold to be **important and right** — the thing I will not sell.
• Attitude is the way I come; value is what I stand on.
• **Good attitudes**: **positive thinking** (I look for what can be done), **cheerfulness** (I come with a bright face), **willingness to help**, **punctuality**, **patience**, and **the readiness to try again**.
• **Bad attitudes**: **complaining** (the mouth is always finding fault), **laziness** (the body will not start), **selfishness** (all for me), **rudeness**, **cheating**, **giving up**.
• An attitude can be **changed** on purpose: the child who says "I cannot" begins to say "I cannot yet", and next month he can.
• The **core values of Nigeria** to name: **honesty, hard work, respect, integrity, patriotism, discipline, tolerance, commitment to excellence, and the spirit of service**.
• **Integrity** is doing right **when nobody is looking**; honesty is telling the truth **when it costs me**.
• **Patriotism** is loving Nigeria: keeping her things clean, respecting her flag, learning her story, and refusing to spoil what belongs to all of us.
• **Tolerance** is letting a classmate pray, eat and greet in his own way while I keep mine.
• Values are taught by **being seen**: the teacher who returns the extra change, the pupil who hands back a found bag, the class that plays without cheating.
• Our class keeps a **value of the week**: one word on the board, one example each day, and Friday is when we say who showed it.

**Words for my notebook**

Attitude is how I think and act; value is what I hold dear. Honesty, hard work, respect, integrity and patriotism are our core values.

4. What does integrity look like when you find money on the floor?

### WEEK 8 — FINDING HELP IN DANGER (PARTS 1 AND 2)

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · SCS · Third Term W2 (Part 1) and W3 (Part 2), pp 659–661 — the school keeps both in one week.*

**You will learn to**

• say what danger is and name its kinds
• tell who can help me and when to run to them
• keep calm, speak clearly and give my name and place
• keep safe from strangers

**Things to know**

• **Danger** is any situation that can **harm** us — the body, the eyes, the breath or the life.
• Kinds of danger a child meets: **fire, road accidents, getting lost, strangers, bad weather (storm, flood, lightning), deep water, electric wire, sharp objects, poison, a mad dog**.
• An **emergency** is a danger that is **happening now** and needs help at once: a burn, a fall with blood, somebody not breathing, a house on fire.
• People who help: the **grown-up nearest**, the **teacher**, a **parent or guardian**, the **police**, the **fire service**, the **doctor or nurse**, the **neighbour** we know, and the **community watch**.
• **Staying calm** is the first rescue: a child who screams cannot be heard; a child who breathes and speaks can be helped.
• Numbers to know (as our scheme sets them, and as they stand today): **112 — the national emergency number, free from any phone, for police, fire or ambulance anywhere in Nigeria**. **767 — Lagos State emergency line (police, fire and ambulance in Lagos)**. **199 — the police emergency line in some states**; **122 — FRSC for road accidents**; **190 — the Federal Fire Service**. The safest thing a child does is dial **112** and say what happened.
• What to say in an emergency call, in this order: **my name → where I am → what happened → how many people are hurt → the phone number I am calling from**. Then **stay on the line** until told to stop.
• **Trusted adults**: the ones at home, the ones in the school, the ones with a name tag at the bank, the shopkeeper we buy from every day, the **police officer in uniform**. If I am lost, I go to a **shop, a filling station or a police post** — never to a car.
• **Stranger rules**: **do not talk to a stranger, do not follow a stranger, do not accept gifts, sweets or rides, do not go into a stranger's house or car. And do not let anybody touch the parts of the body that the swimsuit covers** — say "NO", run, and tell at once.
• **Secrets**: a bad grown-up may say "do not tell anybody"; we tell that kind of secret the same hour. Telling is not tattling.
• Know by heart: **my full name, my home address, my father's and mother's phone numbers, my class and school** — a child who can say these is half-found already.
• **Body danger drill**: fire — get down low, do not run through smoke, use the stairs, never a lift, do not go back for a bag. Road — stop, look both ways, listen, cross at the corner or the bridge; water — never swim alone.
• In a **storm or flood** we move to high ground and stay away from poles, wires and trees; lightning comes first to the tall thing.

**Words for my notebook**

Danger is anything that can harm me. Keep calm, say your name and place, and dial 112. Say no to strangers and tell at once.

### WEEK 9 — SECURE ENVIRONMENT

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · SCS · Third Term W4 (Secure Environment Part 1, pp 659–661).*

**You will learn to**

• say what a secure environment is
• name the things that make a place safe
• find the dangers around my home and school
• report a danger I see

**Things to know**

• A **secure environment** is a place where people are **safe from harm** — body, property and feelings all protected.
• Things that make a place secure: **a fence and a gate, locks on doors, lights that work at night, a watchman or security guard, a dog that is known, windows with bars, and neighbours who know one another.**
• **Feeling safe** matters as much as being safe: at home, at school and in the community a child should not be afraid of the corner, the man or the noise.
• Dangers in our own environment to name: **an open well or pit, a busy road with no bridge, a broken wire on a pole, sharp cutlass or broken glass left on the floor. An uncovered drain, a heap of refuse that breeds mosquitoes, water in a bucket a small child can fall into, a loose wall, a mad dog, a dark passage.**.
• **Rules keep us safe**: close the gate behind you, do not open to a stranger, do not climb the fence. Keep off the road to play, do not touch the wire, do not light a fire in the house, do not go into the water alone.
• **Staying alert** means noticing: the smell of kerosene, the sound of a stranger on the roof, the boy hanging on the gate, the water rising in the street.
• **Reporting a danger** is a duty, not tattling: "the well has no cover", "the glass is on the corridor", "the wire hangs low" — a child who reports prevents an accident.
• At school the safe signs we learn: **no entry**, **mind the step**, **caution wet floor**, **fire exit**, **keep gate closed** — a sign is a rule with a picture.
• Home safety habits: the key is kept in **one place** (not in the lock), the gas is off at night. The matches are high up, the knife is put away, the child does not sleep in a room with a paraffin stove burning.
• A secure place is also clean and lit: **a bright street is a safe street**; a compound with no refuse has no rats and no mosquitoes.
• Our class ends with a **walk of safety**: each child points at one danger on the school ground and one thing that makes the school secure.

**Words for my notebook**

A secure environment is a safe place — fence, lock, light, guard and rules. Report a danger you see; that is good citizenship.
