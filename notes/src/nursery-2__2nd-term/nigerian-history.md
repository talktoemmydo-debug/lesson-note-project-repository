### WEEK 1 — HEROES AND HEROINES IN THE COMMUNITY (PART 1)

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Nigerian History · Second Term W1 (pp 651–652).*

**Main content**

• A **hero** is a person we admire because of what he **does for others**; a **heroine** is a woman who does the same.
• A hero is not somebody with power over people; a hero is somebody with **hands out to people**.
• Three qualities every hero has: **bravery**, **kindness** and **selfless service**.
• **Bravery** is doing the right thing even when the heart is beating fast with fear.
• **Kindness** is a soft heart: helping without being asked, speaking gently, sharing what I have.
• **Selfless service** is work done for others without asking for payment or praise.
• A hero **tells the truth** when telling it is dangerous, and **keeps his word** when keeping it is costly.
• Heroes are also ordinary: the woman who carries a neighbour's water, the man who fills the hole in the road with stones.
• Heroes are **remembered** — with a name on a street, a story told to children, a statue, a day in the calendar.
• A **bully is the opposite of a hero**; so is a thief, and so is a liar, whatever his size.
• In our own community the hero may be the nurse at the primary health centre who stays after closing.
• We say it together: a hero is known by what he gives, not by what he takes.

**Words for my notebook**

A hero or heroine is a person we admire for helping others — brave, kind and selfless.

**Let us talk**

2. Tell me something brave you have done, and something you were afraid to do.
3. Who helps people in our community without pay?
4. Why do people remember heroes?

### WEEK 2 — HEROES AND HEROINES IN THE COMMUNITY (PART 2)

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Nigerian History · Second Term W2 (pp 651–652).*

**Main content**

• A **local hero** is a person who serves the people near us; we can see them, greet them and thank them.
• **Teachers** are heroes: they give knowledge away every day, mark books after class, and stay with a child who has not understood.
• **Doctors and nurses** are heroes: they heal the sick, sit up at night in the clinic, and give medicine without looking at whose child it is.
• **Police officers** are heroes: they keep us from thieves, stand at the crossing, and take those who hurt others to court.
• **Firefighters** are heroes: they run into the smoke when everybody else runs out, and they put out the fire on the house and in the bush.
• Other helpers in the same row: the **midwife**, the **driver** who takes the sick to hospital, the **cleaner** who sweeps the market before dawn, the **water-seller**, the **imam and the pastor** who counsel the quarrelling.
• **Parents and guardians are everyday heroes**: they work, they feed us, they pay school fees, they wake before us and sleep after us.
• Everyday heroes do not wear a special uniform; their heroism is that they **do it again tomorrow**.
• We can thank a local hero: greet them, say "thank you, sir/madam", keep the class clean for the cleaner, do not litter in front of the market woman.
• A hero is also known by the **risk** he takes: the nurse holds the needle, the firefighter holds the pipe, the driver holds the wheel on a bad road.
• Our class will make a wall chart: "Heroes of our community" with one drawing each.

**Words for my notebook**

Teacher, nurse, doctor, police officer, firefighter, parent — all are heroes because they serve us.

**Let us talk**

4. How can we thank the cleaner of the market?

### WEEK 3 — NIGERIAN NATIONAL HEROES (PART 1)

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Nigerian History · Second Term W3 (pp 651–652).*

**Main content**

• A **national hero** is a person whose work was for the **whole country**, not for one family or one town.
• **Herbert Samuel Johnson Macaulay** was born in Lagos in **1864** and died in **1946**.
• His grandfather was one of the first freed slaves returned to Africa; the family lived in Lagos.
• Macaulay was trained as a **surveyor and engineer** — he measured land and mapped towns for the government.
• He left the government service because the colonial rulers would not treat Nigerians fairly, and then he spent his life **fighting for Nigerians with words, courts and papers** instead of guns.
• He founded the **first political party in Nigeria** (the Nigerian National Democratic Party, 1923) — before that, Nigerians had no party of their own to choose leaders with.
• He started a **newspaper** so that the people could read the truth and not only hear what the chiefs were told.
• He led protests against unfair taxes and against the removal of the people's land; he took the people's cases to court.
• That is why he is called the **Father of Nigerian Nationalism**: he first made Nigerians feel like **one people** with one demand.
• Nationalism means loving your country and asking that it be run by its own people.
• Macaulay is remembered in Lagos by a **street name**, and in the story every school child learns.
• Picture study: an old gentleman in a cap and long coat, with mutton-chop whiskers; that is how the photographs of him look.

**Words for my notebook**

Herbert Macaulay (1864–1946) — father of Nigerian nationalism; he formed the first political party and used the newspaper and the court for Nigeria.

**Let us talk**

4. Why is he called the father of nationalism?

### WEEK 4 — NIGERIAN NATIONAL HEROES (PART 2)

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Nigerian History · Second Term W4 (pp 651–652).*

**Main content**

• **Nnamdi Azikiwe** was born in **1904** in Onitsha land and died in **1996**.
• People called him **"Zik of Africa"** — a name the whole continent knew.
• He was a **journalist** who wrote about freedom for black people, and a **student** who read widely and worked hard.
• He founded a **newspaper** (*The West African Pilot*) and used it to teach Nigerians that they could govern themselves.
• He helped to found a political party (**NCNC**) and worked with other leaders to win **independence for Nigeria in 1960**.
• When Nigeria became a republic in **1963**, Azikiwe became the **first President of Nigeria** — the first Nigerian to hold that seat.
• He built a **university** at Nsukka and gave scholarships to Nigerian students; he said education was the way forward for the country.
• Lessons from his life: **read**, **write the truth**, **do not beg for what you can win**, **put the country before your pocket**, **keep on when people say no**.
• Azikiwe spoke of one Nigeria above tribe; he asked that the Igbo, Yoruba and Hausa child sit in the same class.
• His name is on the airport in Abuja, on Nnamdi Azikiwe University, on the 1960s coins and on the street in every city.
• Both our national heroes this term — Macaulay and Azikiwe — fought **with the pen and the platform**, not with a machete.

**Words for my notebook**

Nnamdi Azikiwe (1904–1996), "Zik of Africa", journalist and first President of Nigeria (1963).

### WEEK 5 — NIGERIAN NATIONAL HEROINES (PART 1)

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Nigerian History · Second Term W7 (pp 651–652).*

**Main content**

• A **heroine** is a woman whose bravery and work people remember; Nigeria has many.
• **Queen Amina** ruled **Zazzau** — the city we now call **Zaria** — in the old Hausa land, long ago (she is placed by historians about the **1500s**).
• She was the daughter of a chief and was trained from girlhood: riding, fighting, and leading.
• She became the ruler of her people and led her **own armies** on horseback.
• Amina was a **warrior queen**: she fought to make her kingdom safe and wide, and her cavalry was feared.
• She is remembered for the **city walls** — the long banks and ditches round towns called **ganuwa** — some of which are still standing and were named after her.
• She opened **roads and markets** and made the trade routes safe, so that grain, cloth, leather and iron could travel.
• Under her, Zazzau grew rich and strong; her people were fed in the dry season from what the farms stored.
• The lesson the school takes from Amina: **a woman can lead, plan, build and defend**; she is not a side character in history.
• Girls in the class are told her name for the same reason boys are: heroes are not a male word.
• We look at the picture of the Zaria city walls and count how many gates we can see.

### WEEK 6 — NIGERIAN NATIONAL HEROINES (PART 2)

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Nigerian History · Second Term W8 (pp 651–652).*

**Main content**

• **Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti** (1900–1978) was a teacher from Abeokuta and one of Nigeria's greatest women.
• She led the **Abeokuta Women's Union** — thousands of market women who were angry that unfair taxes were put on them.
• In **1946–1949** she marched, sang and negotiated with the chiefs and the colonial officer until the tax on the women was **taken away**.
• Her people called her the **"Lioness of Lisabi"** and **Iye oja** — mother of the market.
• She wore her **adedaye cloth** and her cap in front of the rulers so that the women's dress became a badge of dignity, not of shame.
• She fought for **women's right to vote** and to be chosen as representatives; she stood for office herself.
• She was the **first Nigerian woman to drive a car**, she studied abroad, and she travelled to many countries for her people.
• **Margaret Ekpo** (1914–2006) of Aba did the same work in the east: she organized the **market women**, taught them to register to vote, and became one of the **first women in the regional and federal House of Representatives**.
• Both women used **peaceful protest, the song and the petition** — they never told their followers to burn or kill.
• Other notable Nigerian women to name: **Queen Moremi** of Ile-Ife, who gave herself to save her town; **Madam Efunroye Tinubu** of Abeokuta and Lagos, a trader and kingmaker. **Professor Grace Alele-Williams**, mathematician and the first Nigerian woman to be a vice-chancellor; and **Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie**, whose books tell Nigeria to the world.
• We celebrate our heroines with a song, a line of writing and one drawing each on the class board.

**Words for my notebook**

Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti led the Abeokuta market women and removed an unfair tax; Margaret Ekpo organized the women of Aba.

4. Name two other notable Nigerian women and one thing each did.

### WEEK 8 — DEVELOPING THE ATTITUDE OF SELFLESS SERVICE (PART 1)

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Nigerian History · Second Term W9 (pp 651–652).*

**Main content**

• **Selfless service** is working for somebody's good while you **look after their need, not your own gain**.
• The test of it: if nobody pays you and nobody thanks you, do you still do it?
• Small acts at school: carry a friend's bag when the strap has cut his hand, share chalk, lend a pencil, hold an umbrella for a smaller child, help a blind pupil across the yard.
• Being **kind to classmates**: greet them, do not call names, include the child sitting alone, do not laugh when somebody answers wrongly.
• **Sharing**: if I have three oranges and three of us, each takes one; if I have one, the one who has none is given half and I do not make a noise about it.
• Simple service at home: fetch water, wash my plate, sweep, carry firewood, fan somebody with heat, arrange the chairs — without being told twice.
• Kindness costs nothing and buys everything; a hard word builds a wall, a soft word pulls it down.
• A selfish child helps only when he is watched; a selfless child helps when he sees the need.
• What selflessness is **not**: doing good to be made class captain, giving so that you are given back, helping only the rich child.
• Service has a **cost** of its own: time, comfort, sometimes the football match I missed while I helped; that paid price is the proof.
• Our class rule for this week: **do one unasked thing every day** and put a tick beside the day in your book.

**Words for my notebook**

Selfless service is helping others without asking for a reward or a thank-you.

**Let us talk**

### WEEK 9 — DEVELOPING THE ATTITUDE OF SELFLESS SERVICE (PART 2)

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Nigerian History · Second Term W10 (pp 651–652).*

**Main content**

• To **volunteer** is to put your hand up and say "I will do it" before anybody asks you.
• Service in the community: the class sweeps the street in front of the school, clears the gutter, plants and waters seedlings, picks plastic from the field.
• **Keeping the environment clean** is service to everybody: the dust that is burned or buried is one less sickness in the street.
• Rules of clean service: refuse in the bin, not on the gutter; broken glass wrapped in paper so the sweeper is not cut; wash hands after the work.
• Helping **elderly people**: give them your seat, carry their load, cross them the road, open the door, do not answer them rudely, sit when they speak and listen to the whole story.
• The aged have time for a child that the busy do not; their stories are our history without books.
• Being a **good citizen** in Nursery 2 language: obey the school rules, pay for what you take, do not cheat in the game, tell the truth at the market, respect the flag, keep public property.
• Public property belongs to **everybody and to nobody** — so a desk with a broken top is somebody's younger brother's problem.
• In a **role play** we practise: one child carries a heavy basin, what do you do? An old man cannot find his way, what do you say?
• Service is taught by being seen: when the class leader picks a paper without complaining, three others do the same on the way home.
• Nigeria is held together by people who serve without looking at tribe or religion; help the child who is not from your father's town first.
• We close with our own pledge: *I will keep my place clean, I will help without being asked, I will not wait to be paid for kindness.*

**Words for my notebook**

Volunteer — offer before you are asked. Clean street, helped elder, kept public property: that is a good citizen.
