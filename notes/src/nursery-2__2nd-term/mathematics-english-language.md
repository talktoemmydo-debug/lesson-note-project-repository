### WEEK 1 — CAPITAL LETTERS

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · Literacy (Language Domain) · Second Term W1 (pp 470–474).*

**Main content**

• Letters come in two sizes: **capitals (big letters)** and **small letters (lower case)** — A is the capital of a, Q of q.
• Our alphabet has **26 capitals**; the Yoruba alphabet has 25 letters and no c, q, v, x or z, so those five capitals are only for English words.
• **Rule one — a capital begins every sentence**: "**T**he sun is hot." · "**I** can read."
• **Rule two — a capital begins a name**: a person's name (****B**ola, **M**usa, **C**hinelo**), a place (**L**agos, **A**buja, **N**igeria), a month or a day.
• **Rule three — the word "I" is always a capital**, wherever it sits: "Ada and **I** went."
• Names to practise today: my own first name, a friend's name, my father's name, my town, my country — each with the first letter big and the rest small.
• Days of the week (the first letters are capitals): **Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, Saturday, Sunday** — Wednesday and Saturday are the two that lose their first sound when we say them fast.
• Months of the year (all begin with a capital): **January, February, March, April, May, June, July, August, September, October, November, December** — twelve, and each one is a name, not a common word.
• Forming the letters: every capital stands on the line and rises to the top. The small letters **g, j, p, q, y** dip their tails **below** the line, and **b, d, f, h, k, l, t** go up like a flag.
• Match the pairs at the board: **A a · B b · C c … Z z** — the shape of some is quite different (**G g, R r, Q q**), and some are only smaller (**C c, S s, X x**).
• Convert: "bola went to lagos" → "**B**ola went to **L**agos." — circle every letter that must be made big.
• Where else we see capitals: the school gate sign, the Bible, the shop board, the front of my own exercise book — the capital letter is a small flag that says *this is important, this has a name*.

**Words for my notebook**

A capital begins a sentence, begins a name, and is used for the word "I". Monday · January · Lagos · Bola.

2. What is the capital partner of "y"? Which small letters go below the line?
3. Write your name and your town with the right big letters.
4. Say the days of the week and then the months.

### WEEK 2 — SIGHT WORDS: ONE, WE, SOME

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · Literacy (Language Domain) · Second Term W2 (pp 470–474).*

**Main content**

• Three new sight words: **one · we · some** — read them with the eye, not by blending.
• **one** = 1 — it is a **word and a number** at the same time: "I have **one** bag." It also means *a single one*: "**One** day I will be big."
• **we** = me and others together: "**we** are in class." — the word that has no "you and I" in it but means exactly that.
• **some** = more than one, but not counted: "**some** pens", "**some** water" — it does not tell us how many, only that there is enough for a little.
• **One car · two cars · some cars** — "one" counts, "some" does not; that is the difference between the number word and the little word.
• Sentences to read: "**We** see **one**." · "**Some** are here." · "**We** have **some**." · "**One** is for **me**, **some** are for **us**."
• Hand signals: one finger for **one**, both arms wide open for **some**, the whole class standing together for **we** — when the word is read, the hand shows the meaning.
• **we** begins with **w** (two V shapes joined) and ends with **e** — do not write "wi" or "wee"; **some** has a quiet **s** at the front and an **e** at the end that is not shouted.
• Sound hunt: which of the three words has the sound /w/? (we) · which ends in the sound /n/? (one) · which begins with /s/? (some).
• Memory game: the three cards face down; turn two, say both words, and if you can say a sentence with them you keep the pair.
• Writing: each word is written **three times** — once small and fast, once big and slow, once in a sentence.
• Counting connection: **one, two, three…** — "one" is the first number we ever say; the word is the number, and the number is a shape (**1**).
• Look for them at home: "one" on a page of a book, "some" on a food packet, "we" in a song — tick each word when you find it.

**Words for my notebook**

one · we · some — "I have one bag." "We are in class." "We have some water."

**Let us talk**

1. Which word means me and you together?
2. Is "one" a number or a word? (Both — say why.)
3. Give me three of something and one of something else; which word describes each?
4. Read this: "Some are here, we see one."

### WEEK 3 — DOUBLE CONSONANTS

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · Literacy (Language Domain) · Second Term W3 (pp 470–474).*

**Main content**

• A **double consonant** is **the same consonant letter written twice together**: ra**bb**it, la**dd**er, o**ff**, e**gg**, be**ll**.
• The twelve to look for: **bb · dd · ff · gg · ll · mm · nn · pp · rr · ss · tt · zz**.
• Words for each pair: **rabbit** (bb) · **ladder, add** (dd) · **off, muffin** (ff) · **egg, giggle** (gg) · **bell, ball, tall** (ll) · **summer, hammer** (mm) · **dinner, banner** (nn) · **happy, puppy** (pp) · **purr, carrot** (rr) · **dress, miss** (ss) · **butter, kettle** (tt) · **buzz, jazz** (zz).
• The big rule to hear: the double consonant makes **one sound**, not two — we do not say "e-g-g"; we say **egg** once and let the **g** be firm.
• The **short vowel** rule (first idea): the double consonant usually comes right after a **short** vowel — **a** in ha**pp**y is short; compare **hoping** and **hopping**: the double tells the ear the o is short.
• Position: a double consonant comes in the **middle** (rabbit, dinner) or at the **end** (off, buzz, fill) — never at the **start** of an English word; that is a good test question.
• Word building with cards: **ra + bb + it** → rabbit; **la + dd + er** → ladder. Now take the double out and the word is wrong — "raler" is not a word: that is why the doubled letter matters.
• Sorting game: write the words in two columns — **has a double consonant** / **has none**: egg · pen · bell · sun · happy · cat · off · ball.
• Spelling check: after I write a word, I look at it and ask *"is there a pair in it?"* — the pairs are the ones we lose most in writing.
• Reading aloud: *"The puppy sat on the ladder and saw the big red ball."* — say every word with a double consonant again, slowly.
• Names can carry a double letter too — **Anna** (nn) and **Rebecca** (cc); if anybody in our class has a name with a pair in it, write it on the wall chart under that pair.

**Words for my notebook**

rabbit · ladder · egg · bell · summer · dinner · happy · butter · dress · buzz — one sound, two letters.

**Let us talk**

1. What is a double consonant?
2. Say the sound in "off" — is it one g or two sounds?
3. Which words have a double: "pen", "egg", "sun", "ball"?
4. Where can a double letter come in a word — the start, the middle, or the end?

### WEEK 4 — SIGHT WORDS: EGG, ADD, INK, COMB

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · Literacy (Language Domain) · Second Term W4 (pp 470–474).*

**Main content**

• The four words: **egg** (what the hen lays) · **add** (to put together — the maths word) · **ink** (the black liquid in a pen) · **comb** (the thing for the hair; and a hive of bees is also a comb).
• **egg** has **gg** and **add** has **dd** — the double consonants of last week are now living inside words I must know whole.
• **ink** is three letters and three sounds: /ɪ/ /n/ /k/ — the **k** after **n** is a firm finish, and it is also the sound of the word ending in "think, drink, pink".
• **comb** has a **silent letter**: the **b** is written but **not said** — we say /kəʊm/, "kome"; the b sleeps at the end.
• Other sleeping letters to name: **k**nee (k silent), **w**rite (w silent), li**gh**t (gh silent), **comb** (b silent) — we say it this way: *English still writes what it used to say, even after the mouth moved on.*
• Why "comb" keeps its **b**: long ago the b was sounded; the spelling stayed when the talking changed — the word's history is written on its back.
• Sentences to read: "**Add** one **egg**." · "The **comb** has **ink** on it." · "Do not put **ink** on the **egg**." · "**We** can **add** the **egg**."
• Use the words together: *What do you add?* (egg) — *What do you comb with?* (comb) — *What is inside the pen?* (ink) — this is the sense-check we do when a word is wrong.
• Write each word four ways: small, big, in the air with the finger, and once inside a sentence of my own.
• Sight-word review: the words learnt so far (Week 5–6 of Letter Work, plus one/we/some, plus today's four) are put on one big chart; I read the chart once forwards, once backwards.
• Sorting: which are **maths or school** words (add, ink) and which are **home** words (egg, comb)? A word can belong to both — say so.

**Words for my notebook**

egg · add · ink · comb. The b in "comb" is silent — we say "kome".

**Let us talk**

1. Which of today's words has a double letter?
2. Why do we not say the b in "comb"?
3. Say a sentence with "add" about your lunch.
4. What is the sound at the end of "ink"?

### WEEK 5 — ALTERNATIVE SPELLINGS (PART 1): ai / ae / ay AND ee / ea

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · Literacy (Language Domain) · Second Term W7 (pp 470–474).*

**Main content**

• **Alternative spellings** = the **same sound** written in **different ways**; English does this a lot, and it is why spelling must be learnt, not only sounded out.
• The **long a** sound is the letter's **own name**: /eɪ/ — as in "day".
• Three ways to write it: **ai** (r**ai**n, tr**ai**n, m**ai**l, w**ai**t, p**ai**n) · **ay** (d**ay**, pl**ay**, s**ay**, tr**ay**, w**ay**) · **ae** (**ae**robics, **ae**rial) — the last one is rare, so know it and do not chase it.
• The position rule to keep: **ai** sits in the **middle** of a word (rain, tail, nail); **ay** sits at the **end** (day, play, say, may).
• The **long e** sound is /iː/ — the mouth wide, a smile sound.
• Two ways to write it: **ee** (tr**ee**, b**ee**, s**ee**, f**ee**t, sl**ee**p, gr**ee**n) · **ea** (**ea**t, s**ea**, t**ea**, r**ea**d, dr**ea**m, l**ea**f, s**ea**t).
• The same sound, different spelling: **see** and **sea** sound alike; **rain** and **reign** sound alike and only the spelling tells them apart.
• Word families for the week: **rain / train / grain** · **play / say / tray / day** · **tree / bee / free / three** · **tea / sea / pea**.
• Read the line: *"The b**ee** s**aw** the r**ai**n, so she f**lew** to the tr**ee** and sat on a l**ea**f."* — say every word with the long a and the long e.
• Choosing practice: fill **ai** or **ay** — r_n → **rain** · d_ → **day** · tr_n → **train** · pl_ → **play** · s_ → **say**. Then say why: the sound at the **end** of the word takes **ay**, the sound in the **middle** takes **ai**.
• Spelling out loud: say the word, then say the letters, then say which team the sound plays for: "play — p-l-a-y — the long a at the end, so **ay**."
• Why one sound has four coats: English came from many languages, and each brought its own spelling habits; the sound is the child, the spellings are the coats.

**Words for my notebook**

Long a: ai (rain) · ay (day) · ae (aerobics). Long e: ee (tree, bee) · ea (eat, sea, tea). Same sound, different spelling.

**Let us talk**

2. Where in a word do we usually put "ai"? Where do we put "ay"?
3. Say three words with "ee" and three with "ea".
4. Spell "train" aloud and say which spelling team the sound plays for.

### WEEK 6 — ALTERNATIVE SPELLINGS (PART 2): igh/y/ie, oi/oy, ou/ow + SIGHT WORDS: BE, WAS, LIKE, HERE

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · Literacy (Language Domain) · Second Term W8 (pp 470–474).*

**Main content**

• The **long i** sound is the letter's own name: /aɪ/ — as in "I".
• Three coats for it: **igh** (h**igh**, n**igh**t, l**igh**t, r**igh**t, f**igh**t) · **y** (m**y**, fl**y**, cr**y**, tr**y**, sh**y**, b**y**) · **ie** (t**ie**, p**ie**, l**ie**, d**ie**).
• The position rule: **igh** sits in the **middle or end** of a word and keeps the **gh** silent; **y** usually comes at the **end**; **ie** is for a short word's end (tie, pie).
• The **oy** sound /ɔɪ/: **oi** in the **middle** (o**il**, c**oi**n, b**oi**l, s**oi**l, p**oi**nt) · **oy** at the **end** (b**oy**, t**oy**, j**oy**, enj**oy**).
• The **ow** sound /aʊ/: **ou** in the **middle** (**ou**t, sh**ou**t, h**ou**se, m**ou**se, bl**ou**se) · **ow** at the **end** (c**ow**, h**ow**, n**ow**, br**own**, sn**ow**).
• Careful: **ow** can also say long o — **snow, grow, low** — so if the word does not sound like "cow", it is the other ow; the ear decides, the rule only helps.
• Four new sight words: **be** (to be: "Be quiet.") · **was** (the past of *is/am*: "I **was** four last year.") · **like** ("I **like** rice.") · **here** ("Come **here**.").
• Sentences to read: "I **like** the n**igh**t." · "She **was** h**ere**." · "Do not cry, b**oy**!" · "The **mou**se came **ou**t of the ho**u**se."
• Choosing spelling in a new word: I hear the sound, then ask *where does it sit?* — middle or end? Then I put the right coat on it.
• Note the trap: **like** has a **silent e** at the end and the **i** says its own name — that is the family of "hide, time, five" and it belongs to this week's sound.
• Write the four sight words and use each in one sentence of my own; the sentences must be true about me.

**Words for my notebook**

**Let us talk**

2. Which do you use at the end of a word: oi or oy? ou or ow?
3. What does "was" tell us that "is" does not? (the past)
4. Spell "night" aloud and say why the h is there.

### WEEK 8 — TRIGRAPHS: are, air, tch, ure + SIGHT WORDS: HAIR, MATCH, PURE

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · Literacy (Language Domain) · Second Term W9 (pp 470–474).*

**Main content**

• A **digraph** is **two** letters making **one** sound (sh, ch, ee, gh); a **trigraph** is **three** letters making **one** sound — **are, air, tch, ure**.
• **are** says /eə/ — the same sound as **air**: c**are**, sh**are**, d**are**, b**are**, squ**are** · h**air**, f**air**, p**air**, ch**air**, st**air**s.
• That is the surprise of the week: **care** and **air** rhyme, so the **e** at the end of "are" does not add a sound of its own — it only helps the spelling.
• **tch** says /tʃ/ — the same sound as **ch**, but it comes **after a short vowel**: ca**tch**, ma**tch**, wa**tch**, ki**tch**en, pi**tch**, fea**tch**er — after a short vowel we write **tch**. After a long vowel or another consonant we write **ch**: beach, lunch.
• **ure** says /ʊə/ ("oor") or /jʊə/ ("yoor"): p**ure**, c**ure**, s**ure**, pi**cture**, na**ture**, fu**ture**.
• The three sight words of the week are made of these trigraphs: **hair** (air) · **match** (tch) · **pure** (ure) — they must be read whole and also understood as patterns.
• Read: *"I comb my **hair**, strike a **match**, and hold a **pure** white shell."* — say each word slowly and name its trigraph.
• Sort by sound, not by spelling: /eə/ — **care, share, air, chair, pair, square** (six words, one sound, two spellings).
• Say the sound and hear the trigraph: in "catch", the **t** and **ch** join into one push of air: /k/ /æ/ /tʃ/.
• Writing practice: complete the word — h__ __ r → **hair**; m__ tch → **match**; p__ re → **pure** — then read the whole word back.
• The trigraph hunt: in the class story book, find one word with **air/are**, one with **tch**, one with **ure**; the class writes them on one line on the board.

**Words for my notebook**

4. Read the sentence about the hair, the match and the shell.

### WEEK 9 — ARTICLES: THE USE OF 'a', 'an' AND 'some' + SIGHT WORDS: THERE, LIVE, FOUR, DO, THEY, GIVE

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · Literacy (Language Domain) · Second Term W10 (pp 470–474).*

**Main content**

• An **article** is the little word that stands before a thing to tell us how we mean it: **a**, **an** and **some**.
• **a** goes before a word that begins with a **consonant sound**: **a** cat · **a** dog · **a** book · **a** ball · **a** university (the **u** here says /j/, a consonant sound, so **a** is right).
• **an** goes before a word that begins with a **vowel sound**: **an** apple · **an** egg · **an** orange · **an** umbrella · **an** hour (the **h** sleeps, so the ear hears a vowel — **an**).
• **some** goes before **more than one** thing or a thing you cannot count: **some** eggs · **some** boys · **some** water · **some** rice.
• The rule is by the **ear**, not the eye: "an **h**onest man" (silent h) and "a **o**ne-way road" (the o says /w/) are the two that make the class laugh and learn.
• One or many? **a** pen (one), **an** ant (one), **some** pens (many) — **a** and **an** never go before a plural; **some** never before a single thing.
• The six sight words: **there** (that place: "Put it **there**.") · **live** (to be alive; and a house: "We **live** in Lagos.") · **four** (4 — the number word, same sound as "for", different meaning) · **do** (the question helper: "**Do** you see it?") · **they** (other people: "**They** are my friends.") · **give** ("**Give** me the bag.")
• Sentences to read and copy: "**Give** me **an** **egg**." · "**They** **live** **there**." · "**Do** you want **some** water?" · "I have **four** pens **there**." · "**There** are **some** boys on the road."
• **there** vs **their**: **there** is a place, **their** is belonging ("**their** bag") — the middle letters are the difference; write "there" with **here** inside it, because *here* is a place too.
• Choice drill at the mouth: I hold up a thing and say the whole phrase — "a pen", "an orange", "some stones" — three times each, quickly.
• End of term writing: five sentences about my family using **live, there, four, they, give** and one article in each; read them to the class and let them hear if an article is wrong.

**Words for my notebook**

a + consonant sound (a dog) · an + vowel sound (an egg, an hour) · some + many or uncountable (some eggs, some water). there · live · four · do · they · give.
