Week 12 is Closing and Week 13 carries the Examination for this subject.
**Week 11 carries a note** because the school's own table prints it as a teaching row
("Exam Preparation"), not as an examination week.
*Depth: this subject is school-generated — there is no NERDC Yoruba scheme to floor it against. The
extent of each week is the three strands the school itself prints (Ẹ̀dẹ̀ · Àṣà · Lítíréṣọ̀). The master
corpus prints Yoruba without tone marks; tone marks are added in the body of the note so that the
child reads the word correctly, and the topic line is left exactly as the school prints it.*

### WEEK 1 — Ede: Didaruko nnkan ayika ile-iwe / Asa: Eran osin ni ile Yoruba / Litireso: Orin akoniniwa

*Depth: school-generated — three strands as printed in the master corpus for N2 S2 W1.*

**You will learn to**

• name the things around the school in Yoruba
• name the animals a Yoruba family keeps and what they are for
• sing the song of the child who knows his mother's face

**Things to know — Ẹ̀dẹ̀ (naming things around the school — dídárúkọ̀ nǹkan ayíká ilé-ìwé)**

• Last term we named things **in** the room; this term we go **round the school**.
• **òde ilé-ìwé** — the school compound · **pápá ìdaráya** — the playground · **_ilé ìwẹ̀** (tóílẹ̀tì) — the toilet
• **ọ̀ṣọ́** — the guard's post · **ọ̀fìn** (kísíìnì) — the kitchen · **ọ̀gbìn** — the garden · **igí** — tree
• **ògiri** — the fence · **ọ̀nà** — the road · **pátákó ìkẹ́ọ̀** — the teaching board · **ìwé** — book
• Say the pattern **"Ní ilé-ìwé wa, a ní ______."** (In our school we have ______.)
• Walking game: we move round the school, and at each place I say one word: *"Èyí ni ọ̀gbìn."*
• Ask and answer: **"Kí ni èyí?"** — **"Èyí ni ..."** · **"Níbo ni ... wà?"** — **"Ó wà ní ..."**
• Spelling of the new words goes in the notebook with one drawing beside each.

**Things to know — Àṣà (the animals of Yorubaland — ẹranko ìsìn ní ilẹ̀ Yorùbá)**

• The school's row prints **eran osin**: **ẹranko ìsìn** — the animals a Yoruba family keeps for the rite, the feast and the honouring of a guest.
• **ewúrẹ́** — goat · **àdìẹ** — fowl · **ọ̀wọ́ọ̀n** — ram · **àgbo** — sheep · **màlúù** — cow · **àyẹ̀yẹ́** — pigeon · **ẹranko ilé** — the animals a house keeps.
• The goat and the fowl are the two animals a Yoruba home can keep in a small yard; they eat scraps and they find their own grain.
• When a child is born, the family **names** him on the eighth day; when a guest of honour comes, the house **kills a fowl** — that is how the animal enters the rite.
• Every animal has its own sound: ewúrẹ́ says **"mèé"**, àdìẹ says **"kòkòrò"** (cock) / **"gèé"** (hen), màlúù says **"moooo"**, ajá says **"grrr — ọ̀ọ̀!"**.
• We are kind to the animals of the house: water before sun, food at the set time, no tying of legs too tight, no beating.
• Some animals are **not** for eating or keeping: ẹ̀kùn (leopard), ẹ̀jọ́ (python), àlùkò (owl) — in Yoruba tradition they belong to the bush and to ìṣẹ́̀gun, and the child keeps away.

**Things to know — Lítíréṣọ̀ (the song the school names: Orin akomoniwa)**

• The row prints the song by its first words, as the school writes it elsewhere: **"Ọmọ tó mọ ìyá rẹ̀ lójú"**.
• *Ọmọ tó mọ ìyá rẹ̀ lójú, á mọ́ bàbá rẹ̀ lójú;* — a child who knows his mother's face will know his father's face too;
• *Ọmọ tó ṣe ìbéèrè, kò sẹ́nu mọ́lẹ̀.* — a child who asks does not end with his mouth shut.
• Meaning: a child who knows where he comes from knows where he is going; we greet because we were taught.
• Sing it twice: once slowly with the hands flat on the chest, once with a step to each line.
• Answer the line: the class calls **"Ọmọ tó mọ̀!"** and I answer **"Ó mọ́ ilé rẹ̀."**

**Words for my notebook**

Òde ilé-ìwé · pápá ìdaráya · ọ̀gbìn · ogiri. Ewúrẹ́ · àdìẹ · ọ̀wọ́ọ̀n · màlúù — ẹranko tí ilé ń tọ́. Ọmọ tó mọ ìyá rẹ̀ lójú.

**Let us talk**

1. Name five things you saw outside the class this morning, in Yoruba.
2. Which two animals can a small yard keep?
3. When does a family kill the fowl?
4. Say the first line of the song and what it means.

**Worksheet**

1. "Pápá ìdaráya" is the A) playground B) garden C) kitchen D) fence
2. The Yoruba for goat is A) agbo B) ewúrẹ́ C) àdìẹ D) màlúù
3. The animal that gives us eggs in the morning is the A) sheep B) goat C) cow D) fowl
4. "Ọmọ tó mọ ìyá rẹ̀ lójú" teaches that a child should A) hide B) run far C) sleep late D) know his own family
5. Fill in: "Ní ilé-ìwé wa, a ní ____." — the missing word is any thing in our school.
6. Name four things around the school in Yoruba and four animals a home keeps.
7. Sing or say two lines of the song and say what the song teaches.

**My own work**

Walk once round the school, write six Yoruba names of things you saw, and under two of them draw the thing.

### WEEK 2 — Ede: Kika alifabeeti H-Q / Asa: Ounje ile wa / Litireso: Ewi kika (ibowo fagba)

*Depth: school-generated — three strands as printed for N2 S2 W2.*

**You will learn to**

• read the letters h to q again, better than last term
• name the food of our home in Yoruba
• say a poem on giving honour to an elder

**Things to know — Ẹ̀dẹ̀ (reading the letters H–Q — kíkà alífábéètì)**

• We met these letters in Week 9 of last term; this week we read them **backwards, forwards and out of order**.
• The line: **h · i · j · k · l · m · n · ń · o · ọ · p** — the row says H–Q, and in the Yoruba alphabet the letters in that range are h, i, j, k, l, m, n, o, p, for **there is no c, q, v, x or z** in Yoruba writing.
• Sound each letter with the seven vowels: **ha · hẹ · he · hi · ho · họ · hu**, then the same for j, k, l, m, n — say one line fast and one line slow.
• Read the words made from these letters: **ilé** (house) · **ìdí** (bottom/reason) · **ọ̀nà** (road) · **omi** (water) · **inú** (inside) · **ọkọ̀** (vehicle) · **òdo** (river) · **ọ̀pọ̀** (many).
• The tricky pair: **o** (oral) and **ọ** (closed) — **òdo** (river) is not **ọ̀dọ́** (young); say both and hear the mouth change.
• Reading rule: eyes on the line, mouth open, one breath per word, no pointing with the finger at the end of the week.

**Things to know — Àṣà (the food of our home — oúnjẹ ilé wa)**

• The food of the Yoruba home is pounded and wrapped: **ìyan** (pounded yam) · **àmàlà** (yam-flour) · **èbà** (garri) · **ẹ̀̀kọ́** (cassava mash, èkọ́) · **túwò** (grain swallow).
• Soups that go with them: **ẹ̀fẹ́** (leaf soup) · **ègúsí** (melon seed) · **ẹ̀wẹ̀dú** (beans pudding soup) · **ògọ́rò** · **árasá** (for the little one).
• Small foods and drinks of the home: **àkàrà** (bean cake) · **àrà** (roasted plantain) · **ìrẹ̀** (corn pap) · **ògì** (pap with groundnut) · **wàìn ọ̀pẹ̀** (palm wine, for the elders only).
• How the food is paired: **ìyan** goes with **ẹ̀wẹ̀dú** or **ègúsí**; **àmàlà** goes with **ógọ́rọ́** or **ẹ̀wẹ̀dú**; **èbà** goes with **ẹ̀fẹ́** or **ègúsí**; **ìrẹ̀** is for breakfast, not for the feast.
• Foods of the farm we name in the same breath: **iṣu** (yam) · **ọ̀̀̀sà** (cassava) · **ìbà** (cocoyam) · **àgbàdọ̀** (maize) · **ìrẹsì** (rice) · **àrà** (plantain).
• In the Yoruba home we wash the hands first, we sit on a mat or stool, and we eat with the **right hand**, three fingers making the spoon.
• Before a big meal the family says **"Ẹ̀ jẹun, ọmọ mi"**; the child answers **"Ọ́ ńlẹ̀, ìyá/bàbá."**
• Our own food is not lesser food: the child who knows the name of his food knows the name of his people.

**Things to know — Lítíréṣọ̀ (saying a poem on honour for elders — èwì kíkà: ìbọ̀wọ̀ fún àgbà)**

• A **ẹ̀wì** is chanted, not read; it goes with the head and the hands.
• *Àgbà kìí rí nǹkan ṣe,* — an elder never sees a thing done wrongly
• *kó jẹ́ ḱ ọ̀dọ́ ṣe é;* — and let the young one do it;
• *Ọ̀dọ́ kìí rí nǹkan ṣe,* — a young one never sees a thing done wrongly
• *kó jẹ́ ḱ àgbà réra lẹ́yìn.* — and the elder be left behind.
• Meaning: the old and the young work together; honour is shared, not only given.
• Ways of honour in Yoruba: **ìdòbì** (the boy prostrates), **ìkúnlẹ̀** (the girl kneels), the two hands cupped low when we pass in front, and standing when the elder sits down.
• We say the poem three times: whisper, then normal, then loud with the action.

**Words for my notebook**

h i j k l m n ń o ọ p · ìyan, àmàlà, èbà, ẹ̀fẹ́, ègúsí · Àgbà kìí rí nǹkan ṣe, kó jẹ́ ḱ ọ̀dọ́ ṣe é.

**Let us talk**

1. Read from h to p without pointing.
2. Which is the Yoruba word for pounded yam?
3. What does a girl do to greet an elder?
4. Say the poem and give its meaning.

**Worksheet**

1. Which letter is NOT used in writing Yoruba? A) q B) j C) gb D) ṣ
2. "Ìyan" means A) pounded yam B) rice C) palm oil D) bean cake
3. A Yoruba girl greets an elder by A) shouting B) running C) kneeling D) prostrating
4. "Àmàlà" is made from A) plantain B) yam flour C) cassava D) maize
5. Fill in: The Yoruba for water is ____.
6. Name five foods of our home and the soup that goes with two of them.
7. Say the poem on honour and write what it teaches in one sentence.

**My own work**

Write the names of the foods eaten in your house this week, and say the poem to somebody at home before you eat.

Draw three foods your family eats and colour them.

### WEEK 3 — Ede: Onka 11-20 / Asa: Ere idaraya / Litireso: Alo pipa (apamo / apagbe)

*Depth: school-generated — three strands as printed for N2 S2 W3.*

**You will learn to**

• count from eleven to twenty in Yoruba
• name the games we play and the words we say while playing
• begin the telling of àlọ́, with its call and its answer

**Things to know — Ẹ̀dẹ̀ (counting 11–20 — ìkà 11–20)**

• From ten (**ẹ́wọ́/ogún** is twenty) we go up with **-lá**, which means "above ten":
• 11 **ọ̀kànlá** · 12 **èjìlá** · 13 **ẹ̀tálá** · 14 **ẹ̀rìnlá** · 15 **àárúnlá**
• 16 **ẹ̀fálá** · 17 **ẹ̀jẹ́lá** · 18 **ẹ̀jọ́lá** · 19 **ẹ̀sánlá** · 20 **ogún**
• The other way of counting, "what is taken from twenty": 19 **òdínlógún** · 18 **èjìlógún** · 17 **ẹ̀tálógún** · 16 **ẹ̀rìnlógún** · 15 **àárún lógún**
• 14 **ẹ̀fálógún** · 13 **ẹ̀jélógún** · 12 **ẹ̀jọ́lógún** · 11 **ẹ̀sánlógún** — the two roads meet at the same number.
• Clap and count: 11 claps for ọ̀kànlá up to twenty claps and a stamp for **ogún**.
• Ask and answer: **"Ogún ló nǐ?"** — **"Bẹ́ẹ̀ni, ogún ni."** (Do you have twenty? — Yes, I have twenty.)
• Use the numbers on things: 12 chalks, 15 bottle tops, 20 steps from the gate to the door.

**Things to know — Àṣà (games — eré ìdárayá)**

• **Eré ìdárayá** means games that make the body happy and strong.
• Games with words in Yoruba: **"Kìké"** (hopscotch) · **"Aro"** (the clapping game, "aro, aro ko lẹ́sẹ̀") · **"Ṣégbé-ṣégbé"** (a counting-out rhyme) · **"Tẹ̀gbẹ̀tẹ̀"** (hide and seek) · **"Igí àti eré"**.
• Games with a ball: **bọ́ọ̀lù** — pass, catch, throw at a mark; games with a rope: **fífo ọ̀pá** (skipping the rope) and **dídè** (jumping over a stretched band).
• We say before a game: **"Ẹ̀ jẹ́ ká bẹ̀rẹ̀!"** and after: **"A ṣe é, ẹ̀ ṣé."**
• Rule of the Yoruba game: the one who is caught becomes "it" without crying; the winner carries nobody's bag.
• The body of the player: knees bend, hands free, head up, the shout is for cheering and not for insulting.

**Things to know — Lítíréṣọ̀ (the telling of àlọ́ — àlọ́ pípá: apamo àti apagbe)**

• An **àlọ́** is a story made for children, and it is **told** (àlọ́ **pípá**), not read.
• The teller opens with a call; the row prints two of them: **"Àlọ́ àpámọ̀"** and **"Àlọ́ àpágbé"**. The class answers with the same breath.
• Call: **"Àlọ́!"** — Answer: **"Àlọ́ ni!"** · Call: **"Àpámọ̀!"** — Answer: **"Ọmọ ẹyẹ́ ló n fò."**
• A àlọ́ has three parts: **ìbẹ̀rẹ̀** (the opening), **àárín** (what happens), **ìparí** (how it ends and what it teaches).
• A short one to tell this week: the **Ìjàpá** (tortoise) who wanted all the food at the feast of the birds, borrowed feathers, flew up, and fell when the feathers were taken back.
• Its lesson: **gbèsè** (greed) spoils a person; the one who takes everything ends on the ground.
• After every àlọ́ we say the closing: **"Òlúkòòlú ni àlọ́ mi, kí n ó r'ọkọ̀ nínú rẹ̀."** (I have told it crookedly — may the carriage be inside it.)

**Words for my notebook**

ọ̀kànlá · èjìlá · ẹ̀tálá · ẹ̀rìnlá · àárúnlá · ẹ̀fálá · ẹ̀jẹ́lá · ẹ̀jọ́lá · ẹ̀sánlá · ogún. Àlọ́ àpámọ̀! Àlọ́ ni!

**Let us talk**

1. Count from ọ̀kànlá to ogún.
2. Which game did you play yesterday, and what did you say while playing it?
3. How do we answer when the teller says "Àlọ́"?
4. What did the tortoise learn at the end of his story?

**Worksheet**

1. The Yoruba for fifteen is A) ogún B) ẹ̀fálá C) àárúnlá D) àárún
2. Ogún means A) twenty B) fifty C) twelve D) ten
3. A story told for children, with a lesson in it, is called A) oríkì B) ẹ̀wì C) àlọ́ D) orin
4. Before a game we say A) "káàrọ̀" B) "a ṣe é" C) "ọ̀ dàbọ̀" D) "ẹ̀ jẹ́ ká bẹ̀rẹ̀"
5. Fill in: 11 in Yoruba is ____ (the "one-above-ten" way).
6. Count from 11 to 20 in Yoruba, then backwards.
7. Tell a short àlọ́ in three sentences and say its lesson.

**My own work**

Count twenty steps from your door to the gate, saying each number in Yoruba, and write the number at which you turned.

Draw the game you play at break time and colour the ball or the rope.

### WEEK 4 — Ede: Alifabeeti P-Y / Asa: Iwa omoluabi / Litireso: Orin kiko (omo rere)

*Depth: school-generated — three strands as printed for N2 S2 W4.*

**You will learn to**

• read and name the letters p to y
• say what an ọmọlúàbí is
• sing the song of the good child with the right actions

**Things to know — Ẹ̀dẹ̀ (the letters P–Y — alífábéètì p sí y)**

• The last of the letters: **p · r · s · ṣ · t · ì · w · y**.
• Say each with the vowels: **pa pe pọ pi po pọ · ra re rọ ri ro rọ · sa sọ ṣa ṣe ṣọ · ta te tọ ti to tọ · wa wọ wi wo wọ · ya yọ ye yi yo yọ**.
• The letter **ṣ** is the one English has not: the tongue is flat and quiet — **ṣọ** (guard) is not **sọ** (fall).
• Letters we do **not** use: c, q, v, x, z; the digraphs we **do**: **gb** and **j**, and the letters with dots: **ẹ, ọ, ṣ** and **ń**.
• Read these words now: **ọkọ̀** (car) · **ọwọ́** (hand) · **ìyàwó** (bride/younger wife) · **ṣùúrú** (a food) · **tutù** (cold) · **ìsàlẹ̀** (below) · **yíyá** (running).
• Write the whole alphabet from a to y in one line, then the seven vowels under it: **a e ẹ i o ọ u**.
• End of the week test: read any line on the chart from p to y without stopping at the dots.

**Things to know — Àṣà (the child of good character — ìwà ọmọlúàbí)**

• **Ọmọlúàbí** is a child brought up well: **ọmọ + ilé + àgbà** — the child of the elder's house, the one people point at and say "that one is well brought up".
• Marks of an ọmọlúàbí: he **greets**, he **does not lie**, he **does not steal**, he **shares**, he **respects the elder and the younger**, he **keeps his word**, and he **works before he plays**.
• The Yoruba say: **"Ọmọlúàbí kìí ṣe nǹkan tí kò yẹ, bí ẹnikẹ́ni kò bá rí i."** — a well-brought-up child does not do a wrong thing even when nobody is watching.
• **"Ìwà lẹ́wàá"** — character is the real beauty; a fine face with a bad temper is an ugly face.
• **"Ọmọ tí a kò kọ́, àgbàlagbà á kà á."** — the child who is not taught, grown-up life will correct, and correction from life is harder.
• A ọmọlúàbí in our class: raises the hand, does not laugh at a wrong answer, picks the paper that is not his, returns the pencil.
• We name one ọmọlúàbí each morning — and it is not always the same child.

**Things to know — Lítíréṣọ̀ (a song to sing — orin kíkọ́: ọmọ rere)**

• A song with an action is learnt faster than a song with only the mouth.
• *Ọmọ rere, ọmọ ilé wa,* — a good child, the child of our home;
• *ó ń kúnlẹ̀, ó ń dọbì;* — he kneels, he prostrates;
• *ó ń jẹun, kò ń jà,* — he eats and does not quarrel;
• *ọmọ rere nìyẹn o!* — that one is a good child!
• Actions: kneel on line two, touch the floor on line two, rub the tummy on line three, thumbs up on line four.
• Sing it in a circle: one child stands in the middle and is pointed at on the last line; next time it is another child — everybody is a good child in one round.

**Words for my notebook**

p r s ṣ t ì w y · Ẹ, Ọ, Ṣ, Ń àti gb, j. Ọmọlúàbí = ọmọ tí a kọ́ ní ilé àgbà. Ìwà lẹ́wàá.

**Let us talk**

1. Read the letters from p to y.
2. Which sounds has English not got?
3. Name five marks of an ọmọlúàbí.
4. Sing the song and show me the action on the second line.

**Worksheet**

1. Which of these letters is NOT used in writing Yoruba? A) ṣ B) v C) w D) ń
2. "Ọmọlúàbí" means a child who is A) rich B) tall C) the oldest D) well brought up
3. "Ìwà lẹ́wàá" teaches that A) money is beauty B) speed is beauty C) clothes are beauty D) character is beauty
4. The Yoruba word for "hand" is A) ẹsẹ̀ B) etí C) ojú D) ọwọ́
5. Fill in: The seven vowels of Yoruba are a, e, ẹ, i, o, ọ and ____.
6. Write the letters from p to y in order.
7. Say two lines of the song of the good child and what each line shows.

**My own work**

Write four things an ọmọlúàbí does at home, and practise the song with its actions until you can sing it twice without stopping.

Draw one polite thing you did this week and colour it.

### WEEK 5 — Ede: Alifabeeti A-Y (leta nla ati kekere) / Asa: Ojuse ebi / idile / Litireso: Orin kiko lori obi

*Depth: school-generated — three strands as printed for N2 S2 W5.*

**You will learn to**

• match the big letter and the small letter, all the way from A to Y
• say the duties of the family in order
• sing a song on the parent and clap the beat

**Things to know — Ẹ̀dẹ̀ (capital and small letters, A–Y — lẹ́tà nlá àti kékeré)**

• The **big letter (lẹ́tà nlá)** is used at the beginning of a name and at the start of a sentence: **Ọ̀lú, Àṣẹ̀, Mo, Bí**.
• The **small letter** is for the rest of the word: **ọ̀lú, àṣẹ̀, mo, bí**.
• Say the pairs aloud in ten: **Aa Bb Dd Ee Ff Gg Gb Hh Ii Jj**.
• Next ten: **Kk Ll Mm Nn ŃŃ Oo Ọọ Pp Rr Ss Ṣṣ**.
• Last: **Tt Ii (with dot: Ìì) Ww Yy** — the line is complete when you have said all twenty-five.
• Tricky ones: **Gb** is one letter, not two — write it **Gb gb**; the dotted letters must keep their dots, for **o** and **ọ** are not the same word.
• Game: I write my own name with the first letter big; then I write it small; then I write my father's name and my town with big first letters.
• A letter that is written well sits on the line and does not fall below it.

**Things to know — Àṣà (the duties of the family — iṣẹ́ ẹbí àti ìdílé)**

• A **dílé** (family) is the people of one house; an **ẹbí** is the wider family — father's brothers and mothers' sisters, the cousins and the grandmothers.
• Duties of the **father (bàbá)**: **ìpèsè** (providing), **ààbò** (protection), **ìnáwó** (paying for what the house needs).
• Duties of the **mother (ìyá)**: cooking (**ìpèsè oúnjẹ**), nursing the little ones, teaching the language, keeping the house in order.
• Duties of the **children (àwọn ọmọ)**: learning (**kíkọ́**), helping (**ìrànwọ́**), respecting (**ìbọ̀wọ̀**), keeping the place clean, and telling the truth.
• Duties of the **grandparents (àgbà)**: advice (**ìmọ̀ràn**), the names and the stories (**ìtàn àti orúkọ**), blessing the children.
• A family where each one carries his own load does not tire; where one carries all, he falls.
• We name our duties in Yoruba with the verb first: **Mo ń fọ aṣọ** (I wash cloth) · **Mo ń ta omi** (I fetch water) · **Mo ń kọ̀wé** (I write).

**Things to know — Lítíréṣọ̀ (a song on the parent — orin kíkọ́ lórí òbí)**

• The song: *Ìyá ọwọ́, Bàbá ọwọ́,* — honour to mother, honour to father;
• *ọwọ́ méjèèjì ló ń gbé wa sókè.* — it is both hands together that lift us up.
• *Bí a bá kó wọn jọ, ilé á dúró;* — when they stand together, the house stands;
• *bí wọ́n bá tì, a ó ṣubú.* — if they fall apart, we fall too.
• Clap the beat: two claps on **ọwọ́**, a rest on the comma, a swing of the arms up on **sókè**.
• The song is not for singing only: at the end we say **"Ẹ̀ ṣé, ìyá. Ẹ̀ ṣé, bàbá."** out loud, once each.

### WEEK 6 — Ede: Oro ati idakeji / Asa: Ikini ni asiko iseje / Litireso: Alo apagbe

*Depth: school-generated — three strands as printed for N2 S2 W6.*

**You will learn to**

• say Yoruba words and their opposites
• greet people when the community gathers
• tell a short àlọ́ àpágbé with the class answering

**Things to know — Ẹ̀dẹ̀ (words and their opposites — ọ̀rọ̀ àti ìdàkejì)**

• An **opposite (ìdàkejì)** is the word on the other side: big and small, hot and cold.
• Pairs to say in one breath: **ńlá — kékeré** (big — small) · **gígùn — kúkúrú** (tall — short) · **jìnnà — súnmọ́** (far — near) · **òkè — ìsàlẹ̀** (up — down)
• **ìwájú — ẹ̀yìn** (front — back) · **ọ̀tún — òsì** (right — left) · **gbígbóná — tútù** (hot — cold) · **rírọ̀ — líle** (soft — hard)
• **funfun — dúdú** (white — black) · **dídùn — títọ́** (sweet — bitter) · **àyọ̀ — ìbànújẹ́** (joy — sorrow) · **rírọ̀ — líle** (soft — hard).
• Game with the body: when I say **ńlá** you stretch your arms wide; when I say **kékeré** you squat; when I say **ọ̀tún** you point right.
• Use the pair in a sentence: **Òkò rẹ̀ ńlá, ṣùgbọ́n àǹfààní rẹ̀ kékeré** — his body is big, but his sense is small (a playful line the class laughs at).
• A pair is learnt better when the two words are said **together, back to back**, ten times in a line.

**Things to know — Àṣà (greeting at a gathering — ìkíini ní àṣíkò ìṣẹ̀jọ́)**

• An **àṣẹjọ (ìṣẹ̀jọ́)** is a meeting of the people: the class assembly, the ward meeting, the family gathering, the church or mosque after service.
• When we come into a gathering we say: **"Ẹ̀ káàbọ̀, ẹ̀ nı́ káàárọ̀"** — and the gathering answers **"Ẹ̀ nı́ kaàárọ̀, ọmọ mi."**
• The order of greeting at a meeting: **the eldest first, then the rest** — a child greets the head of the assembly, not the door.
• Standing is the way of the young in a gathering: we stand till the elder says **"Ìjọ̀kalẹ̀"**, sit down and keep quiet.
• To a person who is speaking we do not add our noise; the Yoruba say **"Àgbà kìí wà ní ojú ọ̀nà, kí ọmọdé tó tẹ̀ ẹ́ sọ́̀"** — the elder is present so the child does not stumble.
• Words to keep at a meeting: **"Ẹ̀ ṣé fún àǹfààní"** (thank you for the chance to speak) · **"Ẹ̀ jọ̀wọ́ ẹ̀ máà gbà mí"** (please receive me) · **"Ẹ̀ ṣé, ẹ̀ jọ̀wọ́."**
• After the meeting, we greet the ones we did not see: **"Ṣé ẹ̀ rí mi lójú ìjọ̀"?** — and the answer is **"Ìméjì"**.

**Things to know — Lítíréṣọ̀ (àlọ́ àpágbé)**

• **Àpágbé** is the family of àlọ́ told to **carry the listener away** into the story — it begins with a nonsense charm so that the ears open.
• Call: **"Àlọ́ àpágbé!"** — Answer: **"Àpágbé lójú, ẹ̀ ṣé!"**
• A short one for this week: the **Ajá** (dog) who wanted the moon; he jumped, fell, jumped again, and the children laughed until he lay down — and the moon stayed where it was.
• The three questions after any àlọ́: *Who was in it? What did they want? What did it teach?*
• We take turns: one child tells the opening and the ending, another says the middle — a story belongs to the class, not to one mouth.

**Words for my notebook**

ńlá—kékeré · gígùn—kúkúrú · jìnnà—súnmọ́ · òkè—ìsàlẹ̀ · ọ̀tún—òsì · gbígbóná—tútù · funfun—dúdú.

**Let us talk**

1. Say the opposite of "kúkúrú".
2. Who is greeted first at a gathering, and what do you say?
3. What do we do when an elder is speaking?
4. Tell the three parts of the story of the dog and the moon.

**Worksheet**

### WEEK 8 — Ede: Pipe iro ede (faweli ati konsonanti) / Asa: Iwa rere (ere ranpe) / Litireso: Aroso oloro geere lori aja

*Depth: school-generated — three strands as printed for N2 S2 W8.*

**You will learn to**

• pronounce the vowels and the consonants the right way
• say what ọ̀rẹ̀ẹ́-ránpẹ́ (kindness) looks like
• say a fast tongue rhyme on the dog with a sharp sound

**Things to know — Ẹ̀dẹ̀ (saying the sounds — pípe ìró ẹ̀dẹ̀: fawẹ̀lì àti kọ́nsọ́nàǹtì)**

• **Fawẹ̀lì** (vowels) are the sounds the mouth makes open, with no lip or tongue stopping them: **a · e · ẹ · i · o · ọ · u** — seven of them.
• Say them long and short: **á à, é ẹ̀** — the mark of the tone changes the word, so the mouth must not be lazy.
• **Kọ́nsọ́nàǹtì** (consonants) are the sounds made with the lips, teeth or tongue closing: **b, d, f, g, gb, h, j, k, l, m, n, ń, p, r, s, ṣ, t, w, y**.
• Lips do the work in **b, f, gb, m, p, w**; the tongue tip in **d, j, k, l, n, r, s, ṣ, t, y**; the nose in **m, n, ń**.
• The three that English children always spoil: **ọ** (mouth open wide and flat), **ẹ** (the mouth nearly flat but open, not "ay"), and **ṣ** (the tongue flat, no buzzing).
• Say the pairs and hear the mouth change: **òdo** (river) — **ọ̀dọ́** (young one) · **wẹ̀** (to be wet) — **wé** (to enclose) · **ṣa** (to weed) — **sa** (to scatter).
• Practice: put the hand on the throat — **m, n, ń, gb** buzz; **s, ṣ, f, t** do not. That is how to tell the sounds apart when you cannot see.
• Reading a sound means writing it: I say a word slowly, and I put a tick for each sound I hear.

**Things to know — Àṣà (kindness — ọ̀rẹ̀ẹ́-ránpẹ́)**

• **Ọ̀rẹ̀ẹ́-ránpẹ́** is the kindness that helps and expects nothing back; it is the best mark of **ìwà rere**.
• Kindness has hands and a mouth: it **carries**, **shares**, **feeds**, **forgives**, and it **speaks gently**.
• The Yoruba say **"Ọwọ́ tí a fi ń tọ́ka, ìyẹn ni yóò fi ń gba"** — the hand that points will also receive; so point at nobody and help everybody.
• **"Kò sí ẹ̀yà tí kò ní ìdí; kò sí ìwà tí kò ní èsì."** — every fruit has a stalk, every behaviour has a return: kindness comes home.
• Kindness in the class: give water to the one who is thirsty, let the smaller child go first, cover the friend who wet himself, do not laugh at a broken word.
• Kindness to the animals too: the dog at the gate is given water, the goat is not beaten with a stick.
• The test of kindness: do it **once** when it is seen, and **twice** when it is not.

**Things to know — Lítíréṣọ̀ (a fast rhyme on the dog — àròsọ olóró gẹ́lẹ́ lórí ajá)**

• An **àròsọ** is a rhyme of hard, sharp sounds said as fast as possible; it trains the mouth, not the memory only.
• The one on the dog:
• *Ajá ọ̀dọ́, ajá àgbà,* — the young dog and the old dog;
• *ó ń sáré, ó ń sọ̀ ẹ̀rùn;* — it runs, it sniffs the wind;
• *ṣe é ṣé kí á ṣàṣìṣe?* — can we be said to have failed?
• *Aṣìṣe kìí ṣe ọwọ́ ajá.* — a dog's paw does not make mistakes.
• Say it in three speeds: slow and clear, medium, then fast three times — the mouth that trips on the first try should not be laughed at.
• Sounds to catch in it: **aj, ágb, sár, ẹ̀rùn, ṣé, ṣà, ọwọ́** — the **ṣ** cluster is the reason the rhyme is called "olóró" (sharp).
• We finish with the class saying the last line together, in a low voice, and then the loud voice — the loud one is the fun.

**Words for my notebook**

Fawẹ̀lì: a e ẹ i o ọ u. Kọ́nsọ́nàǹtì: b d f g gb h j k l m n ń p r s ṣ t w y. Ọ̀rẹ̀ẹ́-ránpẹ́ = kindness with the hands.

**Let us talk**

1. Say the seven vowels and then the consonants made by the nose.
2. What is the difference between the mouth of "o" and "ọ"?
3. Name four acts of kindness in this class.
4. Say the dog rhyme at the fastest speed you can manage.

**Worksheet**

2. Which of these is a nasal consonant (said with the nose)? A) ṣ B) l C) ń D) p
