### WEEK 1 — DEVELOPMENT THROUGH SPORT ACTIVITIES: EMOTIONAL DEVELOPMENT

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · PHE · Second Term W2 (pp 552–557).*

**Main content**

• **Emotions** are the feelings of the heart: joy, fear, anger, pride, disappointment.
• Games and sports move my feelings; that is one reason play teaches more than the leg.
• **Good feelings from play**: **zeal** (I am eager to try), **excitement** (my heart is quick with happiness). **contentment** (I am satisfied just to have taken part), **joy**, **confidence** (I believe I can), **pride** in a thing I have done well.
• **Hard feelings from play**: **anxiety** (nervous before the race), **apathy** (I do not care at all), **anger** (somebody fouled me), **boredom** (this game is not for me), **hostility** (I want to hurt the other side), **disappointment** (we lost).
• Every hard feeling has a way through it: **anxiety is beaten by practice**; **apathy is beaten by choosing a game I like**; **anger is beaten by self-control**. **boredom is beaten by trying something new**; **hostility is beaten by playing peacefully**; **disappointment is beaten by good sportsmanship**.
• **Sportsmanship** means: play fairly, lose quietly, win without insulting, shake the hand of the one you beat.
• Winning and losing are **both part of a game** — the one who has never lost has never really played.
• **Resilience** is the heart coming back: I fall, I am sad, and I still run the next race.
• The body and the feeling work together: when I breathe deep and slow, the fear in the chest goes down; when I jump and shout, the joy grows.
• Feeling talk: it is right to say "I am angry"; it is not right to hit because I am angry.
• After every game we ask two questions: *What did I enjoy?* and *What do I do with what I felt?*

**Words for my notebook**

Play brings joy, excitement, confidence and pride; it also brings fear, anger and disappointment. Managing those feelings is what sport teaches.

**Let us talk**

1. How did you feel the last time your team lost?
2. What do you do when you are angry in a game?
3. Name a feeling you like to have while playing.
4. What does "contentment" mean after a game you did not win?

### WEEK 2 — DEVELOPMENT THROUGH SPORT ACTIVITIES: SOCIAL DEVELOPMENT

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · PHE · Second Term W3 (pp 552–557).*

**Main content**

• **Social development** is learning to live and work with other people; a game is a small town with rules.
• **Cooperation** is working together and helping a team-mate when his line fails.
• **Teamwork** is all of us pushing toward one goal, each knowing his own job: one throws, one catches, one runs.
• **Tolerance** is accepting that another child is slower and I do not laugh; being patient with a mistake.
• **Friendliness** is kind words to team-mate and to opponent; a game makes friends faster than a classroom does.
• **Fairness** is playing by the rules, not cheating, and accepting what the one who judges says.
• **Hospitality** in a game means welcoming the new child who does not know the rules yet, and teaching him instead of sending him out.
• **Respect** is for the one who trains us, for the opponent, for the team-mate, for the equipment and for the rule.
• **Communication** is speaking clearly to my side ("here!", "mine!") and listening when instructions come; shouting nonsense is not communication.
• **Leadership** has two faces: sometimes I lead the line, sometimes I follow — both are needed and neither is bigger.
• These are **life skills**: the child who can wait for his turn in a game can wait his turn at a counter when he is grown.
• Character is built when nobody is watching the ball and I still do not push.

**Words for my notebook**

Cooperation, teamwork, tolerance, friendliness, fairness, hospitality, respect, communication, leadership — a game teaches all ten.

**Let us talk**

1. What is the difference between cooperation and teamwork?
2. A new child does not know the rules: what do you do?
3. When is it your turn to lead, and when to follow?
4. Which social lesson was hardest for you this week?

### WEEK 3 — MARCHING EXERCISE: CONCEPT AND IMPORTANCE

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · PHE · Second Term W4 (pp 552–557).*

**Main content**

• **Marching** is organized walking in rhythm and in a formation — the feet step, the arms swing, everybody moves as one.
• **At attention**: heels together, toes a little apart, body straight, arms at the sides, eyes front, mouth quiet, chest up.
• **Arm swing**: the arm swings from the shoulder, not the elbow; the hand goes to about the level of the waist-belt in front and a little behind.
• **High knee** on the spot: lift the knee toward the chest, put the foot down on the ball, alternate.
• **Forward march**: the left foot starts; the step is short and even; I do not look at my feet.
• **Spacing**: an arm's length to the child in front and to each side, so that no one is kicked and no one is left behind.
• **Commands**: "Attention!" "Forward — march!" "Halt!" "Left turn!" "Right turn!" "About — face!"
• **Turning**: on "Left turn" I turn on the heel of the left foot and the ball of the right, ninety degrees, sharp and without stumbling.
• **About-face** is a half turn to the left, done in one movement, not two.
• Marching teaches **discipline and focus**: the ear listens for the word, the body waits for it.
• It gives **good posture**, **coordination** of hand and leg, **teamwork** (all feet fall on the same beat), and it trains **listening**.
• We march for events: parade, the founding day, the sports fete, a funeral honour, the flag day — marching is how a people show that they are organized and respectful.
• The beat comes from a drum; the left foot falls on the strong beat, so a class of forty makes one sound.

**Words for my notebook**

Attention — forward march — halt. Left foot starts, arms swing from the shoulder, one arm's length between us.

**Let us talk**

1. Which foot starts when we march forward?
2. What does "at attention" look like?
3. Why must there be space between us?
4. Name two events where people march.

### WEEK 4 — BODY EXERCISE: SWINGING, TWISTING, JUMPING AND BENDING

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · PHE · Second Term W7 (Swinging and Twisting) and W8 (Jumping and Bending), pp 552–557 — the school keeps both in one week.*

**Main content**

• **Swinging** moves a body part back and forth, or round and round, without leaving the ground.
• **Arm swings**: forward and back, side to side, big circles front and big circles back; both arms together, then one after the other.
• **Leg swings**: hold the wall or a bench, swing one leg forward and back, then across the body side to side.
• **Trunk swing**: bend a little from the waist and let both arms swing from side to side, touching the ground on each side.
• **Twisting** turns a part round on itself: hands on waist, hips still, shoulders left and right — look over the shoulder each time.
• Neck turns are **gentle and slow**: chin toward the shoulder, never a hard circle. Wrist and ankle rotations go round both ways.
• Swinging **loosens the joints**, twisting **improves flexibility and range of motion**, and both **warm the muscles** before the fast work.
• **Jumping**: two feet together, arms up, land with the knees bent — a **soft landing** means the feet say nothing.
• Kinds of jump: **jumping jacks** (arms and legs out, then in), jump and turn a quarter or half way, jump forward and back, side to side, over a rope on the ground, into a pattern of squares.
• **High jump** reaches up to touch; **long jump** goes forward for distance from a short run and two feet.
• **Bending**: forward bend to touch the toes, side bend left and right with the arm over the head, knee bends (squats) with the heels down and back straight.
• A backward bend is done **only with help and on a soft mat**, never alone and never on a hard floor.
• All movement is **controlled, not jerky**; a bounce that hurts is a sign to stop.
• The four movements go into a routine: warm with swings, turn with twists, be loud with jumps, end with slow bends and a breath.

**Words for my notebook**

Swing, twist, jump, bend. Warm up first, land softly with bent knees, never jerk a movement.

**Let us talk**

1. Which movement loosens the joints first?
2. Show me a soft landing.
3. Why do we not do a hard circle with the neck?
4. Count your jumping jacks aloud — how many did you do?

### WEEK 5 — NEUROMUSCULAR SKILLS: CLIMBING

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · PHE · Second Term W9 (pp 552–557).*

**Main content**

• **Neuromuscular** means **the nerves (brain and messages) working with the muscles**: the eye sees, the brain decides, the muscle moves, and the body keeps balance.
• **Climbing** is moving the body up or over something using hands and feet together.
• Before I move: **three points stay, one point moves** — two hands and a foot on the ladder while the other foot searches for the rung.
• **Hand-eye and foot-eye coordination**: the hand goes exactly where the eye put it; the foot finds the rung without looking down too much.
• **Body awareness** is knowing where my legs are without looking; it is trained by slow climbing, not fast.
• Ways to climb stairs: forward one step at a time, two steps at a time, sideways holding the rail, and backward very carefully with help.
• Ways on the playground: up the **jungle gym** bars, along the ladder, down the slide's steps; **over obstacles** — a mat, a bench at low height, a soft block.
• A **wall climb** is only on a wall made for it, with a mat below and the belt and the hold checked.
• Strength needed: **arms** to hold, **legs** to push (the legs do most of the work), **trunk** to keep the body steady.
• **Problem solving**: I look up first and plan my route — which hand, which rung, where to come down.
• **Confidence and courage**: I climb as high as I am ready; the fear that stops me at a bad hold is a good teacher, and I come down slowly rather than jump.
• **Safety**: only where an adult has said yes, on a mat or sand below, no pushing under me. No climbing with a slick hand, nothing sharp or heavy in the pocket, one child on a rung range at a time.
• Climbing is a **full-body** work; after it we shake the hands and walk the legs before sitting.

**Words for my notebook**

Neuromuscular = brain, nerves and muscles working together. In climbing: three points stay, one moves; plan the route first.

2. Which part of the body does most of the pushing in a climb?

### WEEK 6 — TRACK EVENTS

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · PHE · Second Term W10 (pp 552–557).*

**Main content**

• A **track event** is a race run on a marked path called the **track**; on the day of the sports fete we run in our **lane** and do not cross into another child's.
• Our short distance is the **25-metre race** — about the length of the class hall laid flat.
• The **standing start**: feet one behind the other, weight forward, eyes on the line.
• The **crouching start**: on the marks, hands behind the line, fingers making a bridge, one knee down; at the word the legs push the body out low.
• The signal: "On your marks — set — go!" (or a whistle); I move **only** at the last sound, not before.
• **Running form**: eyes front, mouth a little open, arms bent and swinging from the shoulder, knees lifting, land on the front of the foot, do not twist the body.
• **Do not slow down before the line**: run **through** the finish, ten steps past it, then walk — never stop dead, because the legs must carry the blood back.
• **Rules**: stay in your lane, no pushing, no cutting the corner, no blocking, do the false start again if told, and take the result of the one who judges.
• **Sportsmanship**: greet before, shake hands after, thank the one who lost to you; a win by cheating is a loss.
• In **running in pairs** the two of you run side by side to the line at the same speed; one waits if the other stumbles.
• A harder pair game: the two hold a bean bag or a small stick **between you** without hands and run without dropping it — that is cooperation carrying speed.
• Everybody who runs is celebrated, not only the first: we clap the last child home loudest, because he did not stop.

**Words for my notebook**

Track = the marked path. Move only at "go", run through the line, walk after, shake hands whatever happened.

**Let us talk**

1. How far is our short race?
2. Show me the position of the hands at a crouching start.
3. Why do we not stop dead at the line?
4. What do you do if your partner stumbles in a pair race?

### WEEK 8 — POSTURE

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · PHE · Second Term W11 (pp 552–557).*

**Main content**

• **Posture** is the position in which I hold my body when I sit, stand, move or rest.
• **Good sitting posture**: back against the chair, bottom well back, both **feet flat on the floor**, back straight, shoulders relaxed, head level and not pushed forward.
• Bad sitting: **slouching** (body sunk down, back curved), one leg under the body, chin on the desk, the head bent to one hand.
• Sitting badly **hurts the back and the neck**, makes breathing shallow (the chest is squeezed), and makes it hard to concentrate; the child who looks tired may only be sitting crooked.
• **Good standing posture**: feet about hip-width apart, weight on both feet evenly, knees not locked, stomach gently in, shoulders back and down, head up, chin level, arms loose at the sides.
• Bad standing: leaning on one hip, head forward, shoulders rolled in, knees bent backwards — these tire the legs and bend the spine.
• **Good reading posture**: the book or paper held about the length of a forearm from the eyes, **at a level a little below the eyes**. Back supported, light coming from in front or the side, **not** lying on the stomach or in a dark corner; rest the eyes now and then.
• Reading in bad light or too close pulls the eyes and gives a headache; that is why we do not read while walking or in a moving bus.
• **Good sleeping posture**: on the **side** with the knees a little bent and a pillow between them, or on the **back** with a thin pillow; the **front (tummy)** position twists the neck and presses the chest — avoid it.
• The pillow should not push the head high; the mattress should be firm enough that the back does not sink into a hammock.
• **Carrying posture**: the school bag is worn on **both shoulders** and sits high on the back; a bag hung on one shoulder pulls the spine sideways.
• A simple check: heels, bottom and shoulders touching the wall, eyes front — if the head cannot find the wall without tilting, the sitting of this week has bent it forward.
• Good posture looks **confident** before it feels healthy: the straight child appears ready, and in our mock examination practice we sit tall on purpose.

**Words for my notebook**

Posture = how I hold my body. Feet flat, back straight, eyes an arm's length from the book, bag on both shoulders.

**Let us talk**

1. What is posture?
2. Show me how you should sit when you write.
3. Which sleeping position should we avoid?
4. Why is a one-shoulder bag bad for the back?

### WEEK 9 — INDOOR GAMES

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · PHE · Third Term W1 (pp 558–563).*

**Main content**

• An **indoor game** is a game played **inside a building**, in a room or a hall, that needs **little space** and makes **little noise**.
• We play indoor games when the **weather is bad**: rain, too much sun, or a harmattan so dusty that running would hurt the chest.
• Indoor games exercise the **mind**: thinking, remembering, counting, planning, waiting.
• **Ludo**: a board, a dice and four pieces each; you throw, count the squares, and the piece that reaches **home** first wins — patience and counting, not running.
• **Chess** begins with names: the king, queen, rooks on the corners, knights and bishops, the line of pawns in front; we learn how the pieces move, and the full game comes later.
• **Checkers (draughts)**: men move forward diagonally, capture by jumping, and become kings at the far line.
• **Dominoes**: matching ends with the same number of spots; a child learns to count the pips and to plan two turns ahead.
• **Card games** we can play: matching pairs by number or colour, and the **memory game** with cards face down — turn two, if they match keep them; that game trains the eye and the memory.
• **Puzzles**: putting pieces back into a picture, counting and matching; **Snakes and Ladders** teaches the ladder up and the snake down while we count.
• A **table game** needs a table or a mat; a **video game** needs a screen, and its rules are the screen's: short time, good light, the pause at the eyes.
• Indoor manners: no shouting, do not flip the board when losing, take turns, put the pieces back in the box so the next class can play.
• **Balance**: the body still needs outdoor running; an hour of the mind inside is answered by an hour of the legs outside.

**Words for my notebook**

Indoor games are quiet games for a room: Ludo, cards, puzzles, dominoes, checkers, chess. Mind and legs both need play.

**Let us talk**
