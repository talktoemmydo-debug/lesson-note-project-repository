### WEEK 1 — FARMERS AND THEIR WORK

*Depth: NERDC Primary 4 · Prevocational Studies · First Term W1–W2 (Concept of Agriculture, p 833).*

**You will learn to**

• say who a farmer is
• name the kinds of farmer and the work each does
• tell the daily work of a crop farmer
• say that farming is a job that needs skill

**Things to know**

• A **farmer** is a person who grows crops or keeps animals for food and for selling.
• Not everybody who has a farm is a farmer: the one who **works** the land is the farmer.
• Kinds of farmer: **crop farmer** (yam, cassava, maize, rice, vegetables) · **animal keeper** (poultry birds, goat, sheep, pig, cattle) · **fish farmer** (fish in a pond) · **tree crop farmer** (cocoa, oil palm, rubber).  The **forester** grows and keeps the trees.
• Work of the crop farmer, in order: **clear the land → till and make the beds → choose and plant the seed → weed**.  Then **water or wait for rain → guard against pest and bird → harvest → dry, store and sell**.
• The animal keeper's daily work: **open the house, give water, give feed, clean out the litter, look for the sick one, collect the eggs, close the house at night** so the thief and the dog do not come.
• The fish farmer feeds the fish at set times, keeps the water moving and clean, and nets the big ones out.
• A farmer also **keeps record**: how many bags, how many birds, how much was spent on bag and on medicine.
• Farming work begins early: the farmer is on the farm before the sun is high, when the body is cool and the weeds are soft.
• Farming gives the town its food, the market its money, the factory its raw material — so the farmer works for all of us.
• Tools and machines belong to the next weeks; today we know that **the hand, the plan and the patience** are the farmer's first three tools.

**Words for my notebook**

A farmer grows crops or keeps animals for food and for sale. Clear, plant, weed, water, harvest, sell.

**Let us talk**

1. Who is a farmer?
2. Name three kinds of farmer you know.
3. What does a poultry keeper do every morning?
4. Why does a farmer begin work early?

### WEEK 2 — FARM VISITS

*Depth: NERDC Primary 4 · Prevocational Studies · First Term W1–W2 (p 833) — the farm visit is where the concept of agriculture is seen.*

**You will learn to**

• say what a farm visit is and why we go
• observe and report what I saw on the farm
• keep the rules of the visit
• name the things on the farm I want to ask about

**Things to know**

• A **farm visit** is when the class goes to a farm to **see with the eyes** what was told in the class.
• We go to a **crop farm**, a **poultry house**, a **fish pond**, a **garden**, or the school's own plot.
• Before we start: the bag is left in the class, the feet are shut well, the hat keeps off the sun, and the water flask is filled.
• Rules of the visit: **walk, do not run on the beds; touch nothing without being told; do not pick the fruit; do not chase the birds; stay with the line; ask, then listen**.
• What to look for on a crop farm: the **shape and height of the plant**, the **kind of soil**, how the **rows** are lined, whether there are **weeds**, whether the beds are **watered or dry**. And any **hole or yellow leaf** that shows a pest.
• What to look for in the animal house: the **type of feed**, the **water pot**, the **smell and cleanliness**, the **space per animal**, and whether any animal sits **apart** — that is the sick one.
• What to ask: *When did you plant? What do you feed them? What kills your crop? Where do you sell? What is dear this year?*
• The visit has **three parts**: before (what we will look for), during (what we see and hear), after (what we write and draw).
• Our **report** has: the name of the farm, the day, the person who showed us, three things we saw, one thing we learnt, one question we still have.
• When we come back we **wash the hands and the shoes**, and the mud is not carried into the class.
• A farm visit teaches respect for the labour: the bowl of rice is a year of somebody's back.

**Words for my notebook**

On a farm visit we look, listen and ask; we touch nothing without leave and we write a report after.

**Let us talk**

1. Why do we go to a farm instead of only reading about one?
2. Name two rules of the visit.
3. What on the farm did you want to touch most?
4. What will your report contain?

### WEEK 3 — COMMON FARM TOOLS: HOE AND CUTLASS

*Depth: NERDC Primary 4 · Prevocational Studies · First Term W3–W4 (Simple Agricultural Tools, p 833).*

**You will learn to**

• draw and name the hoe and the cutlass
• say what each tool is used for
• say which part of each tool does the work
• keep the tool safe and store it well

**Things to know**

• A **farm tool** is a thing we hold in the hand to make farm work easier; most of ours are **simple tools** — no engine on them.
• The **hoe** has a **handle** (wood) and a broad flat **blade** at a right angle; it is used for **tilling, making ridges and beds, earthing up the plant and weeding between rows**.
• The blade of the hoe is **pushed into the soil and pulled** — the digging is by the arms and the back, not by a swing.
• The **cutlass** (cutlass, a short heavy blade with a wooden handle) is for **clearing**: cutting grass, brushing small sticks, cutting the weed and slashing the bush.
• A cutlass cuts by a **clean swinging stroke**; the blade must be sharp — a blunt blade needs more force and slips more.
• **Parts of a tool**: **blade** (the working metal part), **edge** (the cutting side), **handle** (the part in the hand), **tang/ferrule** (the joint that holds the blade on the handle).
• A tool with a loose handle must be **fixed before it is used**, or the blade will fly off in the swing.
• Safe use: clear the ground of stones and people first, keep an arm's length of **space** between two workers, never swing toward a person, and never carry a cutlass point-forward.
• Carry a cutlass **blade down and away** from the body, or sheathed; carry the hoe with the blade down.
• Care: wipe the mud off after use, keep the edge dry so rust does not eat it, put a drop of oil on the metal now and then; a rust-eaten tool breaks.
• **Storage**: tools hang on a wall or lie on a rack, **edge down or sheathed**, out of the rain, and never where a child will step on them.
• Sharpening is done with a **file or a stone** by an adult, and the blade is held away from the body.

**Words for my notebook**

Hoe: blade and handle — for tilling, ridging and weeding. Cutlass: a sharp edge — for clearing. Clean, dry, sheathed, hung up.

**Let us talk**

1. Which part of the hoe goes into the soil?
2. What do we use a cutlass for?
3. Why must a tool handle be tight?
4. Where should a cutlass be kept after work?

### WEEK 4 — COMMON FARM TOOLS: RAKE AND WATERING CAN

*Depth: NERDC Primary 4 · Prevocational Studies · First Term W3–W4 (p 833), with Third Term W7 (School Garden Activities) for the watering work.*

**You will learn to**

• name and draw the rake and the watering can
• say what each is used for
• level a seedbed and water a garden the right way
• tell which tool is for the bed and which for the path

**Things to know**

• The **rake** has a long handle and a cross head with **teeth (prongs)**; it is a tool for **gathering, leveling and smoothing**.
• Work of the rake: pull the **stones, sticks and old leaves** out of the bed; break the small clods; **level** the soil after the hoe; **smooth** the surface before seeds are sown; clean the path.
• **Level** means the top of the bed is flat all across, so the water stands evenly and does not run off one side.
• Two kinds: the **garden/leaf rake** with springy teeth, and the **heavy soil rake** with stiff teeth; the light one is ours.
• A rake is not a toy for the shoulder and never used to fight; its teeth catch a hand or an eye.
• A **watering can** is a vessel that carries water and pours it gently; it has a **body**, a **handle** on top and behind, a **spout**, and a **rose (sprinkler head)** full of small holes.
• The **rose** breaks the water into a soft rain so that a small plant is not knocked flat and the seed is not washed out of the ground.
• Without the rose the water comes as a **stream** — that is for filling a tub, not for a seedbed.
• Water in the **morning or evening**; at noon the sun takes the water back before the roots have drunk, and cold water on hot leaves scorches them.
• Water the **soil around the plant**, not the leaf from far above; a wet leaf at night invites disease.
• New seeds and seedlings want a little water **often**; a big plant wants more water **less often** so the roots go down looking for it.
• In the school garden our work is: rake the bed smooth → mark the rows → sow → water with the rose → weed when the leaf is dry → watch growth.
• Care: the can is emptied and left with its lid open and mouth down so it does not rust or smell; the rake is cleared of soil and hung up.

**Words for my notebook**

Rake: teeth for gathering, leveling and smoothing. Watering can: spout and rose for a soft rain, watered in the cool of the day.

**Let us talk**

1. What are the teeth of the rake for?
2. What does the rose on the watering can do?
3. When is the best time to water the garden?
4. Which tool makes the top of the bed flat?

### WEEK 5 — COMMON FARM TOOLS: SPADE AND SHOVEL

*Depth: NERDC Primary 4 · Prevocational Studies · First Term W3–W4 (p 833).*

**You will learn to**

• name and draw the spade and the shovel
• tell the spade from the shovel
• say which work each does best
• dig a hole with a straight back

**Things to know**

• The **spade** and the **shovel** look like sisters: both have a **long handle**, a **D or T grip** at the top and a **broad flat blade** at the bottom. Both are pushed with the **foot** and lifted with the **back and arms**.
• The **spade** blade is **flat and square-ish with a sharp top edge** — made for **digging**, cutting the soil, lifting turves, and for the hard ground.
• The **shovel** blade is **curved and hollow with a pointed or rounded front** — made for **lifting and moving** loose things: soil, sand, ash, grain, manure.
• The rule the farmers say: **a spade digs, a shovel moves.**
• Parts to name: **grip (handle top)**, **handle (shaft)**, **blade (scoop)**, **socket (the cup that joins the blade to the handle)**, **edge (the part that enters the soil)**.
• The soil is first **watered or loosened**; dry hard ground breaks a tool.
• To dig safely: feet apart, **back straight**, bend the **knees**, turn the soil with the hips and not with a twist of the waist; the load is carried close to the body.
• Dig by **slicing a rectangle** and lifting it out, not by hacking a big piece; a clean hole is a deep hole.
• A **round-point shovel** digs in soft ground and moves a heap; a **square-point (transfer) shovel** is the one for grain and sand.
• On a farm both are used to **dig the planting hole or pit**, to **make a heap of manure**, to **cover the seed**, to **open a drain** and to **load** the wheelbarrow.
• Care: wash the mud off, dry it, oil the blade lightly, store it **blade down on a board** or hanging. And never leave it standing in the sun on the bare ground where it rusts and the handle dries and cracks.
• A spade is not for prying stones loose by leverage, and never for hitting a nail.

**Words for my notebook**

Spade: flat blade for digging. Shovel: hollow curved blade for lifting and moving. Straight back, bent knees, load close.

**Let us talk**

1. Which tool has the hollow curved blade?
2. What do we do to the ground before we dig it?
3. Show me how you bend to lift a heavy scoop.
4. How do we keep a spade from rusting?

### WEEK 6 — COMMON FARM TOOLS: WHEELBARROW AND BASKET

*Depth: NERDC Primary 4 · Prevocational Studies · First Term W3–W4 (p 833).*

**You will learn to**

• name and draw the wheelbarrow and the basket
• say what each carries on the farm
• load and push a barrow without spoiling the back
• mend and keep them well

**Things to know**

• A **wheelbarrow (barrow)** is a **one-wheeled cart** with a box (tray), two handles and two legs; one person pushes it and it carries the load of many.
• Parts of a barrow: **wheel and tyre**, **frame**, **tray (box)**, **handles (shafts)**, **legs (stands)**, and sometimes a **axle pin**.
• The wheel at the front makes it easy to turn and to lift over a stone; the legs let it stand down without tipping.
• A barrow carries: **manure, soil, sand, water in a drum, harvested crop, bags, stones, waste** — from the heap to the bed, from the farm to the gate.
• A **basket** is a **woven carrier** made of **palm frond, raffia, bamboo, cane or plastic**; it is carried on the **head**, on the **back with a strap**, on the **hip** or in the **hands**.
• A basket carries **yam tubers, cassava, tomatoes, peppers, eggs, maize in a bag, leaves and grass** — the harvest of a small farm.
• Baskets come in sizes: the small **hand basket**, the big **market basket**, the **winnowing basket** with a wide flat rim used to fan chaff from grain.
• Loading a barrow: put the **heavy thing near the wheel** and over it, not at the back handles — that is the difference between light and heavy to lift.
• Balance the load, do not heap above the rim, keep the top level so nothing falls on the path.
• Pushing: stand between the handles, back straight, knees soft, walk with small steps, look where the wheel is going, and on a slope keep the load **uphill** so it does not run away with you.
• A barrow is **not for carrying people**, and never for two children at once on a slope.
• Care: the tyre is pumped, the bolt tightened, the tray cleared of wet soil so it does not rust; a basket with a torn rim is **re-woven**, a split handle is bound with wire or new cane.
• Empty barrow upside down on a dry floor; baskets stacked in a shade, not in the sun which makes the cane brittle.

**Words for my notebook**

Barrow: one wheel, a tray and two handles — heavy near the wheel. Basket: woven carrier for the harvest, worn on head or back.

**Let us talk**

1. How many wheels does a wheelbarrow have?
2. Where do we put the heavy part of the load?
3. What is carried in a basket on our farm?
4. What do we do when a basket rim tears?

### WEEK 8 — IDENTIFYING AND DIFFERENTIATING FARM TOOLS

*Depth: no NERDC line is anchored here — the school's own row for this week is followed, using the Primary 4 tool weeks (p 833) as the vocabulary ceiling.*

**You will learn to**

• pick a tool from its shape alone
• say the name of the tool and its work in one breath
• tell two tools apart by the part that does the work
• sort the tools into the right groups

**Things to know**

• **Identifying** means knowing a thing by its marks; the marks of a tool are its **shape, its material, its working part and its work**.
• Look at the **working part** first, not the handle.  **Teeth** → rake · **flat blade at a right angle to a short handle** → hoe · **long handle with a broad flat blade** → spade · **curved scoop** → shovel · **long blade with a wooden grip** → cutlass.  Then: **wheel and tray** → barrow · **woven body** → basket · **can with a spout and a rose** → watering can.
• Sort by **work**: for digging — hoe, spade; for clearing — cutlass, rake; for carrying — barrow, basket, head pan; for watering — watering can, knapsack sprayer.
• Sort by **the part of the plant or soil it touches**: soil tools (hoe, spade, shovel), plant tools (cutlass, watering can), carrying tools (barrow, basket).
• Sort by **material**: metal and wood (hoe, cutlass, spade, shovel), plastic or metal sheet (watering can), woven plant fibre (basket), rubber and metal (barrow wheel).
• Sort by **the strength it needs**: back-and-leg tools (spade, shovel), arm tools (hoe, rake), hand tools (cutlass), no-force tools (barrow, basket) — the machine takes the weight.
• Names to keep away from confusion: a **spade** is not a **hoe** (the spade is pushed with the foot in line with the handle. The hoe is pulled at a right angle); a **shovel** is not a **spade** (the shovel moves, the spade digs); a **rake** is not a **hoe** (teeth, not blade).
• Our game: the bag holds five cut-out tool shapes; one child draws one and names it and its work before the class may say "right".
• A tool can also be known by **how it is held**: two hands (spade, shovel, hoe), one hand (cutlass), pushed not held (barrow), carried not held (basket on the head).
• If you cannot name a tool, describe it: what it is made of, the part that does the work, and where the farmer stands when he uses it.
• We end with the sound of each word said once, then the class calls the tool for its work: "What do I use to level the bed?"

**Words for my notebook**

Know a tool by its working part: teeth, blade, scoop, edge, wheel, weave, rose. Same job means same group.

**Let us talk**

1. Which part of a tool should you look at first?
2. Tell me a tool you would use for carrying and one for clearing.
3. How is a hoe held differently from a cutlass?
4. Name two tools that are easy to confuse, and the one difference.

### WEEK 9 — DIFFERENCES BETWEEN SIMILAR TOOLS

*Depth: NERDC Primary 4 · Prevocational Studies · First Term W3–W4 (p 833) — differences between similar tools and proper storage.*

**You will learn to**

• tell similar tools apart in one sentence
• say when each of the pair is the better choice
• name the difference in shape, in hold and in work
• say how both are stored

**Things to know**

• **Spade and shovel**: the spade's blade is **flat and stiff** with a sharp top edge so it can **cut into the ground**; the shovel's blade is **curved and hollow** so it can **hold a loose load**. Dig with a spade, move with a shovel.
• **Hoe and spade**: the hoe's blade is **at a right angle** to the handle and works by **pulling**; the spade's blade is **in line** with the handle and works by **pushing with the foot**.
• **Hoe and hand fork (cultivator)**: the hoe has a blade for slicing weeds and ridging; the fork has **three or four prongs** for loosening soil around a plant without cutting the roots.
• **Rake and hoe**: both are pulled, but the rake **gathers with teeth** and leaves the bed smooth; the hoe **cuts with a blade** and turns the soil over.
• **Cutlass and machete**: both are long blades; a cutlass is **shorter and heavier** for slashing bush, a machete is **longer and lighter** for clearing cane and grass in clean strokes.
• **Watering can and knapsack sprayer**: the can carries **water to the root**; the sprayer carries a **fine mist of medicine or liquid fertilizer to the leaf**; the sprayer's tank is pressurized by pumping and its lance has a nozzle.
• **Wheelbarrow and head pan (basket)**: the barrow **rolls** a big load over distance; the pan or basket **is carried** into rows too narrow for a wheel.
• **Bucket and basin**: the bucket is deep for carrying water; the basin is wide and shallow for soaking seeds or washing crop.
• **Sickle and cutlass**: the sickle is a **curved blade in one hand** for reaping grass and maize; the cutlass is a straight heavy blade for the bush.
• Even when the pair looks the same, ask: **which part moves the soil and which part carries it?** That answer nearly always picks the tool.
• **Storage of both of a pair**: the same rule — clean, dry, edge protected, hung or racked, kept out of children's reach and away from the rain; a tool stored wet is a tool that will be bought twice.
• Tools that are **sharpened together and stored apart** keep their edges; a cutlass lying on a spade dulls both.

**Words for my notebook**

Similar tools differ in one part: flat blade or hollow scoop, pulled or pushed, teeth or edge, carried or rolled.

**Let us talk**

1. Which one would you take to lift sand — spade or shovel? Why?
2. How does a hoe move the soil compared with a spade?
3. What is the difference between a watering can and a sprayer?
4. Why must tools not be stored wet?
