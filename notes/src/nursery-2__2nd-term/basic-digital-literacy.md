### WEEK 1 — CHANGES IN SIZE, SPEED AND COST OVER TIME

*Depth: NERDC Primary 4 · Basic Digital Literacy · First Term W2 (History of Digital Devices, pp 827–828).*

**You will learn to**

• say that machines became smaller and faster as time went on
• name the abacus as an old counting machine
• tell that the first computers were as big as a room
• say that a thing once too dear for anybody is now in many hands

**Things to know**

• The **abacus** is a frame with beads on wires; people slide the beads to count, long before any computer.
• **Charles Babbage** is called the father of the computer — he made the plan of a counting machine.
• **Ada Lovelace** wrote the steps for that machine — the first programme, and the first person to see that a computer could do more than count.
• A **generation** is one stage of a machine's life; the computer has passed through **five generations**.
• 1st: **vacuum tubes** — big glass bulbs that ate power and gave out heat. 2nd: **transistors** — small, cool, they made machines smaller.
• 3rd: **integrated circuits** — many tiny parts put on one little chip. 4th: **microprocessors** — the whole brain of the computer on one chip.
• 5th: **artificial intelligence (AI)** — a machine that can learn, answer questions and recognize a face or a voice.
• Over time, three things kept changing: **size** went down, **speed** went up, **cost** per job went down.
• The first computer filled a whole hall and cost more than a house; the phone in a shop today is faster than that hall of machines and thousands of people can afford one.
• Things got smaller: room → cupboard → table → lap → hand → wrist.
• Words for counting speed: a machine does a **million** sums in a second; my finger does one in a second.
• Small, fast and cheap is why a machine that only officers had is now in the hands of traders, farmers and children.

**Words for my notebook**

Old: big, slow, dear. New: small, fast, cheaper. Babbage planned the computer; Ada Lovelace wrote its first programme.

**Let us talk**

1. What did people use to count with before machines?
2. Which was bigger — the first computer or today's phone?
3. Name one thing that has become cheaper over time.
4. What does a machine of the fifth generation do that old ones could not?

### WEEK 2 — HARDWARE AND SOFTWARE

*Depth: NERDC Primary 4 · Basic Digital Literacy · First Term W3 and W4 (Parts of Digital Devices I and II, pp 827–828).*

**You will learn to**

• tell hardware from software
• name the parts I can touch and the parts I cannot touch
• say what the CPU, the memory and the store are for
• say that the two must work together

**Things to know**

• **Hardware** is every part of the computer I can **touch**: screen, keys, mouse, box, wires.
• **Software** is the part I **cannot touch** — the instructions and the pictures inside; it is what tells the hardware what to do.
• I can break hardware with a stone; I cannot spill water on software.
• A **program** (an **app**) is a piece of software: the drawing app, the counting app, the music app.
• Inside the box are four parts to know: the **CPU**, **RAM**, the **hard drive (storage)** and the **motherboard**.
• The **CPU** is the **brain** — it does the work and gives the orders.
• **RAM** is **temporary memory** — it holds what I am doing now; when power goes off, what is in RAM is gone.
• The **hard drive** is **permanent memory** — it keeps my drawing and my story until I ask for them again.
• The **motherboard** is the big board that **connects everything**, the way the road joins the houses.
• The computer also has a **power supply** (it brings the electricity in) and a **cooling fan** (it carries the heat out, or the machine becomes too hot to work).
• Input devices bring work **in**: keyboard, mouse, microphone, scanner, webcam. Output devices take work **out**: monitor, printer, speakers, headphones.
• The parts work as one team: I press a key (input) → the CPU thinks (work) → the screen shows it (output).
• A machine with no software is like a schoolbag with no book in it.

**Words for my notebook**

Hardware is what I touch; software is what tells it what to do. CPU = brain, RAM = now, hard drive = later.

**Let us talk**

1. Point to three pieces of hardware on this computer.
2. Can you hold software in your hand? Why not?
3. Which part is the brain?
4. What happens to what is in RAM when the power goes off?


![The parts I can hold: the box, a blank screen, the keyboard, the mouse on its cord, one disc and one floppy case on the table.](assets/img/nursery-2/2nd-term/basic-digital-literacy/week2-parts-i-can-touch.png)

### WEEK 3 — INPUT DEVICES: KEYBOARD AND MOUSE

*Depth: NERDC Primary 4 · Basic Digital Literacy · First Term W8 (Input Devices, pp 827–828), with W3–W4 for care.*

**You will learn to**

• find letters and numbers on the keyboard
• say what the special keys do
• click, double-click, drag and drop with the mouse
• care for the keyboard and the mouse

**Things to know**

• The **keyboard** is for typing; its letters are not in ABC order but in the **QWERTY** layout — the first six letters on the top row of letters.
• Keys to know: **letters**, **numbers**, **Space bar** (makes a gap), **Enter** (goes to the next line / says "go"), **Backspace** (takes out what is before the mark), **Caps Lock** (makes big letters).
• The letters should be typed with the fingers, not pecked one by one with two thumbs.
• The keyboard is kept **flat and clean**; nothing is placed on it, and no food or drink is beside it.
• Crumbs in the keys make them stick; the keyboard is turned upside down and shaken gently, or wiped with a dry cloth.
• The **mouse** is for pointing; on it are a **left button**, a **right button** and a **wheel** between them.
• **Click** = press once (to choose). **Double-click** = press twice fast (to open). **Right-click** = show a little list of choices.
• **Scroll** = roll the wheel up and down to move the page without pressing anything.
• **Drag and drop** = hold the left button on the icon, move the mouse, let the button go — the icon follows my hand.
• The **touchpad** on a laptop does the mouse's work: slide one finger to move, tap to click. A **touchscreen** answers the finger directly.
• A mouse with a ball underneath is turned over and the ball wiped clean when it stops moving well; an optical mouse only needs its glass wiped.
• The cable is held, not pulled; a yanked wire breaks the socket inside.

**Words for my notebook**

Keyboard: keys for typing. Mouse: click, double-click, scroll, drag and drop. No water near either.

**Let us talk**

1. Show me the space bar and say what it does.
2. Which key takes out a wrong letter?
3. What must I do to open a picture: click or double-click?
4. Show me drag and drop on the screen.


![A child sits straight on the stool with one hand on the mouse, blank keys in front and one empty square on the screen.](assets/img/nursery-2/2nd-term/basic-digital-literacy/week3-one-hand-on-the-mouse.png)
**Worksheet**

1. The arrangement of letters on a keyboard is called the ____ layout. A) ABC B) QWERTY C) Friday D) number
2. The key that moves us to the next line is A) Esc B) Caps Lock C) Enter D) Shift
3. To open a folder we usually A) click once B) shake the mouse C) double-click D) pull the cable
4. Holding the button, moving the mouse and letting go is A) printing B) drag and drop C) saving D) scanning
5. Fill in: The key that makes all the letters big is ____.
6. Mention three things that spoil a keyboard.
7. Write two sentences on how you move a picture from one place to another on the screen.

**My own work**

### WEEK 4 — THE MICROPHONE

*Depth: NERDC Primary 4 · Basic Digital Literacy · First Term W10 (Computer Care and Safety, pp 827–828) for the care lines, with W3–W4 for the device itself.*

**You will learn to**

• say what a microphone is for
• show how voice goes in and comes out
• handle the microphone well
• sit well and rest the eyes when I work

**Things to know**

• A **microphone** is an input device that takes **sound in** — my voice, a song, a hand-clap.
• It changes sound into something the machine can keep, so I can hear it again, or send it to a friend.
• Things that use a microphone: the **phone** (calling), the **recorder**, the **radio**, the **computer** for voice typing, the **microphone on a stage**, a **headset**.
• Speak **across** the microphone, not **into** it — a hard "p" and "b" blow pop sounds into it.
• Keep it about the width of two fingers from the mouth; hold it by the body, never by the cable.
• Care: never drop it, keep it from water, from dust and from being pulled; a microphone that has fallen must be shown before it is used again.
• With earphones the sound goes **in** as well as **out**: I speak, I hear myself — that is how a recording is checked.
• Sound must not be too loud: loud sound in the ear hurts the small parts inside and can spoil hearing for life.
• **Ergonomics** is sitting well: back straight against the chair, both feet on the floor, the screen level with the eyes, arms loose, keyboard flat.
• The eyes rest too: every little while, look far away out of the window for a count of twenty, and blink.
• **Lab rules**: one to a machine unless told otherwise, no food or drink, hands clean and dry, ask before the power is switched on, switch off at the end and push the chair in.
• A **surge protector** saves the machine when lightning or a fault sends too much power along the line; the plug is held by the hard part, not the wire.
• Virus protection means: do not put in a flash drive that is not yours, and do not click strange pictures that say you have won something.
• Shut down the right way — the machine closes itself; pulling the plug while it is thinking spoils the work in RAM.

**Words for my notebook**

A microphone takes sound in. Hold it by the body, speak across it, keep it dry. Sit straight, screen at eye level.

**Let us talk**

1. Which kind of device is a microphone — input or output?
2. Why do we not shout into the microphone?
3. Show me how you sit when you use the computer.
4. What do we do before switching a machine off?


![A child sits upright at the table and speaks quietly into a microphone on its stand, with three thin lines carrying the voice.](assets/img/nursery-2/2nd-term/basic-digital-literacy/week4-speak-quietly-into-the-microphone.png)
**Worksheet**

1. A microphone is used to A) cool the CPU B) print paper C) store files D) take sound in
2. We hold a microphone by its A) body B) cable C) shadow D) plug pin
3. Sitting well while we work keeps our A) shoes clean B) hands wet C) back straight D) bag closed
4. The small rest our eyes need is to look A) far away B) at the floor C) nearer D) down

6. Mention four rules of the computer lab.

### WEEK 5 — SCANNER, WEBCAM AND OTHER PERIPHERALS

*Depth: NERDC Primary 4 · Basic Digital Literacy · First Term W8 (Input Devices) and W7 (Peripheral Devices, pp 827–828).*

**You will learn to**

• say what a scanner and a webcam do
• tell a scanned picture from a photographed one
• name the plugs that join a device to a machine
• say what a peripheral is

**Things to know**

• A **scanner** takes in a **flat paper thing** — a drawing, a letter, a photo — and puts it into the computer as a picture.
• The paper lies face-down on the glass; the light bar travels under it once; the cover is closed so daylight does not wash the picture out.
• A **webcam** is a small camera on top of the screen; it takes in **moving pictures and my face** for a call, not paper.
• Scanner = still and flat; webcam = live and moving. A phone camera is like a webcam I can carry.
• Use of a scanner: to keep a drawing, to send a homework, to put a birth certificate on paper of a computer, to copy a picture into a story.
• Care: the glass is wiped with a soft dry cloth and never with water; nothing heavy is stood on the lid; the paper is flat, no clip or pin on the glass.
• A **peripheral** is an outside device **connected** to the computer to give it more power.
• Peripherals I know: **printer, scanner, external hard drive, USB flash drive, webcam, speakers, mouse, keyboard, headset**.
• They join by **USB** (the small rectangular plug for most things), **HDMI** (the wide flat plug for picture and sound to a television) or **Bluetooth** (joining with no wire at all).
• A **flash drive** is small, carries my work in my pocket, and is pulled out only after the machine says it is safe — pulling it while it writes spoils the file.
• With Bluetooth I must first put the device in **pairing** so the two find each other; then it works from across the room.
• The cable is unrolled, not wound tight round the plug, and the plug goes in the right way — force bends the pins.

**Words for my notebook**

A scanner takes in paper; a webcam takes in my face and the room. A peripheral is a device joined by USB, HDMI or Bluetooth.

**Let us talk**

1. Which device would you use to put your drawing into the computer?
2. Where does the paper lie on the scanner?
3. Name a peripheral that needs no wire.
4. Why must a flash drive not be pulled out while it is writing?


![A scanner with its lid open and one blank sheet on the glass, a small webcam perched on a blank screen, and one plug with a coiled cord.](assets/img/nursery-2/2nd-term/basic-digital-literacy/week5-the-scanner-and-the-webcam.png)

### WEEK 6 — OUTPUT DEVICES: THE MONITOR

*Depth: NERDC Primary 4 · Basic Digital Literacy · First Term W9 (Output Devices, pp 827–828), with W3–W4 for care.*

**You will learn to**

• say what a monitor shows me
• tell the screen from the box
• keep the screen clean and my eyes safe
• say what "resolution" means for a picture

**Things to know**

• The **monitor** (also called the **screen** or **display**) is the output device that **shows** me the work with pictures and words.
• It shows only what the computer has already done — it does not do the thinking; the box does that.
• A **television** is like a big monitor for the sitting room; a **monitor** is for the computer on the table.
• A screen is measured across, in **inches**: the class machines are about the size of a book opened wide; the television at home is much wider.
• **Resolution** is how many small dots make up the picture: many dots give a **sharp** picture, few dots give a **blurry** one with blocks.
• **LCD** screens are flat and light; the old heavy ones (CRT) were deep boxes with a curve on the glass.
• Care: switch off when leaving, wipe with a **soft dry cloth** in a circle, never spray water or detergent on the glass (it runs down into the edge and kills it).
• Do not press a finger on the glass, do not knock it, do not hang on it; a crack inside cannot be mended, only the whole screen changed.
• Keep the screen **out of the sun's bright line** and away from a window's glare, so I can see it without squinting.
• The top of the screen should be about the level of my eyes and an arm's length away; that is why the stand can be raised or lowered.
• Brightness is turned down in a dark room and up in daylight; too much light on the eyes makes the head ache.
• A **projector** is an output device too — it throws the picture big onto a wall for the whole class to see.

**Words for my notebook**

The monitor shows the work. Many dots make a sharp picture. Wipe with a dry cloth, never with water.

**Let us talk**

1. Is the monitor an input or an output device?
2. What makes a picture look blurry?
3. Show me how high the top of the screen should be for you.
4. What must we never spray on a screen?

### WEEK 8 — THE PRINTER

*Depth: NERDC Primary 4 · Basic Digital Literacy · First Term W10 (Computer Care and Safety, pp 827–828) for care, with W9 for the device.*

**You will learn to**

• say what a printer does
• tell an inkjet printer from a laser printer
• put paper in the tray without crumpling it
• care for the printer and the ink

**Things to know**

• A **printer** is an output device that puts the work from the screen **onto paper** so that I can hold it.
• An **inkjet printer** squirts tiny drops of **liquid ink** on the page; it is good for my coloured drawing and it is the common home printer.
• A **laser printer** uses **powder (toner)** and heat to burn the picture onto the paper; it is fast, gives sharp black words, and is the kind in the school office.
• Steps of printing: the work is sent → the machine wakes → paper is pulled up one sheet at a time → the page comes out and dries.
• Paper goes in **flat** in the tray, against the guide, and not too many sheets at once — too many makes the machine **jam**.
• A **jam** means the paper is stuck inside; the machine is switched off and the paper pulled out **slowly with both hands** so no bit tears off inside.
• **Ink/toner is a cartridge**: it is handled gently and kept away from the fingers and the eyes; a black smear on the shirt is hard to wash.
• Do not open the printer while it is moving; the inside parts are hot in a laser printer.
• Quality: choose the paper type — plain paper for words, coated paper for the coloured picture; wrong paper gives a wet or a pale print.
• A printer left on a shelf gathers dust on the rollers; it is covered when not in use.
• Before printing I **look at it on the screen first** (a preview) — printing by mistake wastes paper and ink.
• The spare page from a wrong print is not dust: the back is used for rough work.

**Words for my notebook**

A printer puts work on paper. Inkjet uses liquid ink; laser uses powder and heat. Too much paper in the tray causes a jam.

**Let us talk**

1. What comes out of a printer?
2. Which printer is better for a coloured drawing?
3. What do we do when the paper sticks inside?
4. Why do we look at the picture on the screen before printing?

### WEEK 9 — SPEAKERS AND HEADPHONES

*Depth: NERDC Primary 4 · Basic Digital Literacy · First Term W10 (Computer Care and Safety, pp 827–828) for care and volume, with W9 for the devices.*

**You will learn to**

• say what a speaker and a headphone are for
• know that both are output devices
• keep the sound at a safe level
• care for the small parts that make the sound

**Things to know**

• A **speaker** is an output device that sends **sound out** of the machine so the room can hear it.
• A **headphone** (or **earphone**) also sends sound out, but only **one person** hears it — the sound goes straight into the ear.
• Sound comes from a thin part that **vibrates** (moves to and fro very fast) and pushes the air; that push is what the ear catches.
• The **volume** control makes the sound bigger or smaller; too loud is not more fun, it is damage.
• Loud sound for a long time hurts the ear for life — the ear does not grow back what the noise killed. So: low volume, short time, and I can still hear somebody speaking next to me.
• **Do not share earphones** — the ear passes small dirt and infection from one person to another.
• Care: speakers hate water; the headphone is not wound tight round the plug but looped loosely; the cable is not pulled over the desk edge or under a chair leg.
• The small holes of the earphone are kept clean and free of fluff; a blocked hole sounds quiet and tempts me to raise the volume.
• Speakers come in pairs — one for the left and one for the right — so that I know which side a sound came from.
• Sound is also made by the machine's internal **buzzer**; if the sound comes from the phone or the monitor, that is called a **built-in speaker**.
• Music in class is used for time and for movement: it starts, it stops, and when it stops the class is quiet — that is the rule for the speaker.
• If nothing is heard, first check the volume and the plug, then tell the person in charge of the machine; do not open it.

**Words for my notebook**

Speakers fill the room; headphones serve one ear. Loud sound for long hurts the ear. Keep them dry and clean.

**Let us talk**

1. Is a speaker an input or an output device?
2. Why should we not share earphones?
3. How do you know the sound is too loud for you?
4. What do you do first when no sound comes at all?
