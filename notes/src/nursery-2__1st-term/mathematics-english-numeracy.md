### WEEK 1 — WHOLE NUMBERS 1–200

*Depth: NERDC Primary 2 · Mathematics · First Term W1 and W2 (pp 675–676); Nursery 3 · Numeracy · Second Term W7.*

**You will learn to**

• count from 1 to 200 without losing my place
• read and write numbers 1–200 in figures and in words
• say how many hundreds, tens and units are in a number
• compare two numbers with >, < and =
• count in 2s, 3s, 5s and 10s

**Things to know**

• Counting 1–100 is what I already know; this week we go on to **101, 102 … 200**.
• **100** is one hundred. **200** is two hundred — it is **two hundreds**, or **twenty tens**.
• Every number has a place: **hundreds, tens, units (ones)**. In **147** the 1 is one hundred, the 4 is four tens, the 7 is seven units.
• **Expanded form**: 147 = 100 + 40 + 7. The word "expanded" means opened out.
• Reading a number written in words: "one hundred and forty-seven" → **147**; "one hundred and ninety-one" → **191**; "two hundred" → **200**.
• Comparing: **>** is greater than, **<** is less than, **=** is equal to. The open mouth faces the bigger number: 147 **>** 74.
• Ordering 147, 102, 200, 96 from smallest to largest: **96, 102, 147, 200**.
• Skip counting: by **2s** — 2, 4, 6 … 20; by **5s** — 5, 10, 15, 20; by **10s** — 10, 20, 30 … 200.
• **Even** numbers end in 0, 2, 4, 6, 8; **odd** numbers end in 1, 3, 5, 7, 9.
• Counting is easier when I touch one object for one number: twenty caps, twenty sticks, twenty beads.
• We celebrate it: **"I can count to two hundred!"**

**Words for my notebook**

147 = 100 + 40 + 7. The places are hundreds, tens and units. 147 > 74. Counting by 10s: 10, 20, 30 … 200.

**Let us talk**

1. Count from 95 to 105.
2. In the number 152, which digit is the tens?
3. Say 100 + 60 + 3 as one number.
4. Which is bigger, 189 or 198?
5. Count by fives from 5 to 50.

### WEEK 2 — PLACE VALUE

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 1 · Numeracy · Second Term W2 and Third Term W2 (tens and ones), reinforced by Primary 2 · First Term W1–W2.*

**You will learn to**

• group objects into tens and ones
• say the tens and ones in a two-digit number
• use a tens-and-ones chart
• build a number from its tens and ones

**Things to know**

• **Ten ones make one ten.** That one sentence is the whole lesson.
• Tie sticks in bundles of ten: three bundles and four loose sticks = **34**.
• A place-value chart has two columns: **Tens | Ones**. Put 3 beads under Tens and 4 under Ones → **34**.
• In **47**: the 4 is in the **tens** place and means 40; the 7 is in the **ones** place and means 7.
• The same digit means different things in different places: in **77** the first 7 is 70, the second 7 is 7.
• Ten frames help me see it: two full frames and six more = 26.
• Build it backwards: 5 tens and 2 ones = **52**; 9 tens and 0 ones = **90**.
• Compare with place value first: 61 and 16 use the same digits, but 61 is bigger because it has more tens.
• Round numbers: 30, 40, 50 are 3 tens, 4 tens, 5 tens — the ones column is empty (zero).

**Words for my notebook**

Ten ones make one ten. In 47, the 4 is tens (40) and the 7 is ones. Same digits, different places, different numbers.

**Let us talk**

1. How many ones make one ten?
2. Show me 27 with bundles and loose sticks.
3. In 63, which digit is in the ones place?
4. Which is bigger, 52 or 25, and why?

### WEEK 3 — WRITING NUMBERS IN WORDS

*Depth: NERDC Primary 2 · Mathematics · First Term W1 (pp 675–676); Nursery 3 · Numeracy · Second Term W1.*

**You will learn to**

• write the names of numbers from 1 to 100 in words
• spell the tricky number names
• read a number name and write the figure
• match figures to words

**Things to know**

• 1 **one**, 2 **two**, 3 **three**, 4 **four**, 5 **five**, 6 **six**, 7 **seven**, 8 **eight**, 9 **nine**, 10 **ten**.
• The teens are the hard ones: 11 **eleven**, 12 **twelve**, 13 **thirteen**, 15 **fifteen**, 18 **eighteen**, 20 **twenty**.
• Note the spelling: thirteen (not *threeteen*), fifteen (not *fiveteen*), eighty (not *eighty*).
• From 21 upward we write with a space or a line: **twenty-one**, **thirty-four**, **ninety-nine**.
• 40 is **forty** — there is no "u" in it. 100 is **one hundred**; 200 is **two hundred**.
• Big ones: 147 is **one hundred and forty-seven**.
• Reading test: "sixty-five" → I write **65**; "one hundred and three" → **103**.
• Write it small and keep it on the line; a figure written upside down (6 for 9) is a mistake in sums, not in handwriting.
• Even and odd still work in words: "forty" ends in an even figure, "thirty-seven" in an odd one.

**Words for my notebook**

twenty-one · thirty-four · forty · fifty · one hundred and forty-seven. Spell "forty" without a "u".

**Let us talk**

1. Say the number names from eleven to twenty.
2. How do you spell 40 in words?
3. What figure is "sixty-five"?
4. Say the difference between "thirteen" and "thirty".

### WEEK 4 — WRITING WORDS IN FIGURES

*Depth: NERDC Primary 2 · Mathematics · First Term W1 (pp 675–676).*

**You will learn to**

• turn a number name into figures quickly
• hear a number and write it
• not mix up -teen and -ty
• check what I wrote

**Things to know**

• "Writing words in figures" is the opposite of last week: **thirty-six → 36**.
• Say the word in parts: **sixty-three** = sixty (60) + three (3) → **63**.
• The "-teen / -ty" trap: **thirteen** is 13, **thirty** is 30; **fifteen** is 15, **fifty** is 50. Listen for the long "ee" sound in -teen.
• Zero keeps a place: "two hundred and five" → **205**, not 250 and not 25.
• Write in figures only — no words, no commas — and put one number per line so it can be checked.
• Dictation: I hear the number twice; the first time I listen, the second time I write.
• Checking: read my figure back aloud as a word; if it does not sound like what I heard, try again.
• Matching game in class: the word card and the figure card go together on the wall.

**Words for my notebook**

sixty-three → 63. Two hundred and five → 205. Read your answer back to check it.

**Let us talk**

1. Write "seventy-two" in figures.
2. What is the difference between 13 and 30 in words?
3. Write "one hundred and eight" in figures.
4. How do you check your answer after writing it?

### WEEK 5 — ADDITION (INTRODUCTION)

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Mathematics · First Term W7 (pp 618–619).*

**You will learn to**

• say that addition means putting together
• use the signs + and =
• add within 5 in two formats
• tell an addition story with objects

**Things to know**

• **Addition** means **putting together** or **adding more**; the answer is called the **sum** or **total**.
• The **plus sign (+)** means "and" or "more"; the **equals sign (=)** means "is the same as".
• With counters: 2 stones and 1 stone → count them all → **2 + 1 = 3**.
• Horizontal form: **3 + 2 = 5**. Vertical form: write 3, put +2 under it, draw a line, write 5.
• Number bonds of 5: 0 and 5, 1 and 4, 2 and 3 — pairs that make five.
• Adding **zero** changes nothing: 4 + 0 = 4.
• Counting on is faster than counting all: for 4 + 2, start at 4 and count on two — 5, 6.
• A story problem: *"Ada has 2 oranges, and her friend gives her 1 more. How many has she now?"* → 2 + 1 = **3**.
• Our fingers are the first calculator; the number line is the second.

**Words for my notebook**

Addition is putting together. The sign is +, and the answer after = is the total. 3 + 2 = 5.

**Let us talk**

1. What does the + sign mean?
2. Show me 2 + 3 with your fingers.
3. What is the sum of 4 and 1?
4. Tell a story that ends in 5 + 2.

### WEEK 6 — ADDITION

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Mathematics · First Term W8 (pp 618–619); Primary 2 · First Term W7 and W8.*

**You will learn to**

• add within 10 with objects and pictures
• add two single digits in my head
• add 2-digit numbers without carrying
• use a number line to add
• check an addition with subtraction

**Things to know**

• **Addition facts to 10** are the things I must know without counting: 6 + 4 = 10, 7 + 3 = 10, 8 + 2 = 10, 9 + 1 = 10.
• Adding **zero**: 9 + 0 = 9; adding one: 9 + 1 = 10.
• On a **number line**: to do 6 + 3, put the finger on 6 and jump three steps right → 9.
• Two-digit without carrying: 23 + 14 → add the ones (3 + 4 = 7), add the tens (20 + 10 = 30) → **37**.
• With carrying (a challenge for later in the week): 28 + 7 → 8 + 7 = 15, so 20 + 15 = **35**.
• Adding **three or more** numbers: 2 + 3 + 4 — take two first (2 + 3 = 5), then 5 + 4 = 9.
• **Mental addition**: "What is 5 and 5? 10. Then 10 and 2? 12." No fingers needed.
• Real life: how many in all — 6 pencils and 5 crayons = **11 items**; ₦5 and ₦10 = **₦15**.
• Check with subtraction: 23 + 14 = 37, and 37 − 14 = 23. If the check does not come back, do the addition again.
• The order does not matter: 4 + 6 is the same as 6 + 4.

**Words for my notebook**

Know the facts to 10. For 23 + 14: ones first (3+4=7), then tens (20+10=30) → 37. Check with subtraction.

**Let us talk**

2. Show me 5 + 4 on the number line.
3. How do you add 20 + 30 quickly?
4. How do you check that 25 + 12 = 37?
5. Give me two pairs that make 10.

### WEEK 8 — SUBTRACTION (INTRODUCTION)

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 2 · Numeracy · Third Term W1 (pp 344–347); Primary 1 · Mathematics · First Term W9 (pp 618–619).*

**You will learn to**

• say that subtraction means taking away
• use the minus sign (−)
• subtract within 5 with objects and pictures
• tell what zero does in subtraction

**Things to know**

• **Subtraction** means **taking away**, **removing**, or finding the **difference** between two numbers.
• The **minus sign (−)** means "take away"; the answer is called the **difference** or the **remainder**.
• With counters: 5 stones, take away 2, count what is left → **5 − 2 = 3**.
• Words for the same thing: "5 **minus** 2", "5 **less** 2", "take 2 **away from** 5".
• Horizontal form: **5 − 1 = 4**. Vertical form: 5 on top, −1 below, line, then 4.
• With pictures: cross out the ones that go; count the ones not crossed.
• **Zero in subtraction**: 5 − 0 = 5 (nothing taken, nothing lost); 5 − 5 = 0 (all taken, none left).
• Counting back is the trick: for 6 − 2, start at 6 and count back two — 5, 4.
• A subtraction story: *"I have 5 sweets and I eat 2. How many are left?"* → 5 − 2 = **3**.
• Subtraction is the opposite of addition: if 2 + 3 = 5, then 5 − 3 = 2.

**Words for my notebook**

Subtraction is taking away. The sign is −, and the answer is the difference. 5 − 2 = 3.

**Let us talk**

1. What does the − sign mean?
2. Show me 4 − 1 with your fingers.
3. What is 3 − 3? What is 3 − 0?
4. Tell a story that ends in 5 − 2.

### WEEK 9 — SUBTRACTION

*Depth: NERDC Primary 1 · Mathematics · First Term W10 (pp 618–619); Primary 2 · First Term W9 and W10 (pp 675–676).*

**You will learn to**

• subtract within 10 fluently
• subtract 2-digit numbers without borrowing
• borrow when the ones are not enough
• check a subtraction with addition
• use subtraction with money and with objects

**Things to know**

• **Subtraction facts within 10**: 10 − 4 = 6, 10 − 7 = 3, 9 − 5 = 4 — these must be quick.
• Subtracting zero changes nothing: 8 − 0 = 8.
• "Difference between" means: how far apart two numbers are — the difference between 7 and 3 is **4**.
• Without borrowing: 48 − 25 → ones (8 − 5 = 3), tens (40 − 20 = 20) → **23**.
• With borrowing: 42 − 15 → the 2 cannot take the 5, so borrow one ten: 12 − 5 = 7, and 30 − 10 = 20 → **27**.
• On the number line, subtracting means jumping to the **left**.
• Checking with addition: 48 − 25 = 23, and 23 + 25 = 48. If it does not add back, work again.
• Real life: ₦10 − ₦4 = **₦6** change; 20 pupils, 3 absent → **17 present**; a 60 km journey, 25 km covered → **35 km left**.
• Never swap the numbers without thinking: 3 − 8 is not 8 − 3; in subtraction, order matters.

**Words for my notebook**

For 48 − 25: ones first, then tens → 23. Check by adding back. If the ones are too small, borrow a ten.

**Let us talk**

1. What is 10 − 6? 9 − 4?
2. Find 57 − 23 in your book.
3. When do you need to borrow?
4. Your mother has ₦50 and buys ₦30 of bread — what is left?
5. How would you check 41 − 16?
