### WEEK 1 — COLOURING A CAR

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · Creativity · First Term W1 (pp 564–566).*

**You will learn to**

• know the parts of a car that I can see on paper
• colour inside the lines without going out
• choose the colours I want for my car
• say what a car does for people

**Things to know**

• A car is a means of transportation: it carries people and things from place to place.
• The parts of the car on my colouring paper: **body, wheels, windows, doors and headlights**.
• Any colour is a good colour for a car — the colour I choose is my own idea.
• Windows are often blue or black; wheels are black or grey; headlights are yellow.
• Cars come in different types: **sedan, SUV and sports car**.
• A picture looks complete when I add a background: the road under it, the sky above it, and trees beside it.
• Colouring inside the lines makes my work neat; finishing the whole page makes it complete.

**Words for my notebook**

A car has a body, wheels, windows, doors and headlights. A car carries people and things.

**Let us talk**

1. Point to the wheels on your car.
2. What colour did you give the windows, and why?
3. Name one other thing that carries people.
4. What did you draw under the car?

### WEEK 2 — THUMB PRINT AND HAND PRINT ART

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · Creativity · First Term W4 (pp 564–566).*

**You will learn to**

• press my thumb and my hand on paper to make pictures
• make a caterpillar, a butterfly, a fish or a flower from prints
• add details after the paint has dried
• wash my hands well after the activity

**Things to know**

• A thumb print is made by dipping the thumb in washable paint or an ink pad and pressing it on paper.
• Many thumb prints in a line make a **caterpillar**; two joined make a **butterfly**; small ones scattered make a **ladybug on a leaf**; three in a row can make a **fish**.
• A hand print is made by painting the whole palm and fingers and pressing the hand flat on the paper.
• A hand print can become a bird, a fish, a tree or a person with fingers as legs and hair.
• Every thumb print is different — mine is not like my friend's; that is what makes my picture mine.
• After the paint is dry, I add eyes, legs and lines with a marker or a pen.
• More than one print can make a whole scene: a garden, a pond, a family.
• When I finish, I wash my hands with soap and water.

**Words for my notebook**

I dip, I press, I lift. My thumb print is unique — nobody has another one like it.

**Let us talk**

1. What do we dip the thumb in?
2. Show me your butterfly or caterpillar.
3. Name one thing you made with your hand print.
4. What must be dry before I use a marker?


![A caterpillar, a butterfly, a fish and a flower made from prints.](assets/img/nursery-2/1st-term/cultural-and-creative-arts/week2-print-art-shapes.png)
**Worksheet**

1. A thumb print is made with A) a ruler B) chalk C) water only D) paint
2. Many thumb prints in a line can look like a A) car B) house C) ball D) caterpillar
3. Before I add details with a marker, the paint must be A) cold B) dry C) wet D) hot
4. Every thumb print is A) square B) unique C) the same D) blue
5. Fill in: After the activity I wash my ____ with soap.
6. Name three pictures you can make with thumb prints.
7. Write two sentences on the picture you made in class today.

**My own work**

Make six more thumb prints on a spare page and turn three of them into animals.

### WEEK 3 — COLOURING BARNEY

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · Creativity · First Term W8 (pp 564–566).*

**You will learn to**

• colour a character inside its outline
• keep the body colours the character is known for
• add a background to my picture
• colour carefully and neatly to the end

**Things to know**

• Barney is a **purple dinosaur** in a children's programme; his belly and his back details are **green**.
• A character picture has an outline — the lines that show where the body, the arms and the legs end.
• Colouring inside the outline keeps the character looking like itself.
• I start with the biggest part (the body), then the smaller parts (the belly, the hands, the feet).
• A background makes the picture complete: a playground, a park, or the sky with clouds.
• Characters also live in books, on cards and on screens; a colouring page takes one of them onto my paper.
• Neat work is finished work: no white patches left inside, no colour outside the lines.

**Words for my notebook**

Barney is a purple dinosaur with a green belly. I colour inside the lines and finish my whole page.

**Let us talk**

1. What colour is Barney's body?
2. What did you draw behind him?
3. Show me one place where you stayed inside the line.
4. Why should we not leave white patches?

### WEEK 4 — COLOURING A PIG

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 2 · Creativity · First Term W9 and Nursery 3 · Creativity · First Term W9 (pp 412–414, 564–566).*

**You will learn to**

• name the parts of a pig I am colouring
• choose the colours a pig can be
• colour a curved shape neatly
• say two things we get from farm animals

**Things to know**

• Parts of a pig: **snout** (the long nose), **curly tail**, **round body**, **small ears** and **hooves**.
• A pig is a farm animal; it lives in a pen on the farm with goats, sheep and hens.
• Pig colours: **pink** is the most common; some are **brown, black or white**.
• Details that make the picture real: two eyes, two nostrils on the snout, and hooves on the legs.
• A muddy background, a barn or a fence tells where the pig lives.
• We learn about farm animals through art: the shape of the body, the size of the ears, the kind of legs.
• Farm animals give us food, and they are looked after every day.
• Curved lines are harder to colour inside than straight ones; I go slowly on the round back and the snout.

**Words for my notebook**

A pig has a snout, a curly tail, a round body and hooves. I colour inside the curved lines.

**Let us talk**

1. Point to the snout on your picture.
2. What colour did you make the pig?
3. Where does a pig live?
4. Name one other farm animal.

### WEEK 5 — STRAW JEWELRY MAKING

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · Creativity · First Term W10 (pp 564–566).*

**You will learn to**

• cut straws into small bead-sized pieces with help
• thread the pieces onto a string
• make a pattern that repeats
• tie a knot so that my necklace does not open

**Things to know**

• The things I need: **drinking straws cut into small pieces, string or yarn, and scissors**.
• The straw pieces are as small as beads, so the necklace can lie flat around my neck.
• Straws are cut into bead-sized pieces with a grown-up guiding the scissors.
• Threading: I push the string through one piece, then the next, keeping them in a line.
• A pattern is a repeat: red, blue, red, blue — or red, red, yellow, red, red, yellow.
• The same pieces can make a **necklace** (longer) or a **bracelet** (shorter).
• A knot at both ends, tied with help, keeps the work from falling apart.
• Patterns are everywhere: on cloth, on beads, on the floor tiles; my necklace is a pattern I made.
• Rolling, pushing and threading between my fingers make my hands clever for writing.
• The jewelry I made can be worn, and it can be a gift for somebody I love.

**Words for my notebook**

I cut, I thread, I repeat a pattern, I tie a knot. My necklace is wearable art.

**Let us talk**

1. Show me the pattern you repeated.
2. How many colours did you use?
3. What keeps the beads from falling off?
4. Who helped with the scissors, and why?

### WEEK 6 — COLOURING AN UMBRELLA

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 2 · Creativity · First Term W10 and Nursery 3 · Creativity · Second Term W1 (pp 412–414, 568–571).*

**You will learn to**

• name the parts of an umbrella
• mix bright colours together without fear
• make stripes, dots or sections on my umbrella
• colour a curved outline neatly

**Things to know**

• Parts of an umbrella: the **canopy** (the round top), the **handle**, and the **spokes** inside the canopy.
• An umbrella keeps rain off us, and it keeps the strong sun off us too.
• Bright colours look good on an umbrella — red, blue, yellow, green and orange can sit together.
• Patterns I can make: **stripes** from the top to the edge, **polka dots**, or **sections** where each part gets its own colour.
• Colour combinations: yellow goes with blue, red goes with green; two colours in one section make it look designed.
• The background can carry **rain, raindrops, clouds** — or a shining sun if the day is hot.
• The curved edge of the canopy is where neatness shows; I colour slowly near the curve.
• Finishing the whole page — canopy, handle and background — makes the work complete.

**Words for my notebook**

An umbrella has a canopy, spokes and a handle. It keeps rain and sun off us.

**Let us talk**

1. Point to the canopy on your picture.

3. What did you draw in the sky?
4. Name two colours that sit well together in your work.

### WEEK 8 — VEGETABLE PRINT ART

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · Creativity · Second Term W2 (pp 568–571).*

**You will learn to**

• use vegetables as stamps
• know the shape each vegetable makes
• dip, press and lift without smearing
• clean up after the activity

**Things to know**

• Vegetables can be stamps: an **okra cut across prints a star**, a **carrot prints a circle**, a **potato** can be carved into shapes, and **bell pepper, cabbage and celery** print their own patterns.
• The vegetable is cut for us by a grown-up — we do not use the cutting blade ourselves.
• I dip the cut face of the vegetable into paint, then press it on the paper and lift it straight up.
• Pressing hard and moving the stamp smears the print; a firm, still press gives a clean mark.
• Different vegetables can be combined: okra stars on cabbage circles, carrot rings in a line like a road.
• Prints can be made into a greeting card, a wrapper design, or a picture of a garden.
• The shape a vegetable already carries is what makes its print different from another's.
• When I finish, I wash the stamps or put them where they belong, wipe the table and wash my hands.
• Art can come from food and from nature; the same plant that feeds us can also print.

**Words for my notebook**

Okra makes a star, carrot makes a circle. I dip, press and lift — then I clean up.

**Let us talk**

1. Which vegetable made a star in your work?
2. Show me a print that came out clean.
3. What do we do after printing?
4. Name one thing you can make with vegetable prints.

### WEEK 9 — PLASTICINE (ADVANCED)

*Depth: NERDC Nursery 3 · Creativity · Second Term W3 (pp 568–571).*

**You will learn to**

• roll, flatten, pinch and coil plasticine
• build a figure that has several parts
• mix two colours to make a new one
• make a scene with more than one object

**Things to know**

• Four movements with plasticine: **rolling** (a ball or a snake), **flattening** (a pancake), **pinching** (ears, a nose) and **coiling** (a snail's shell, a rope).
• A figure with parts: a **person** with a head, a body, two arms and two legs; the limbs can be joined loosely so they move.
• A detailed animal has ears, a tail, legs and eyes; a vehicle has a body, wheels and windows.
• Small pieces go on big pieces: make the body first, then add the smaller parts.
• Two colours pressed together make a new shade: red and yellow make orange; blue and yellow make green; black and white make grey.
• A scene holds more than one object: a house with a car and a tree; a farm with animals and a farmer.
• Building makes me think in three ways: what sits under, what sits on top, what will fall.
• Too much pressing spoils the shape; I hold my work from the bottom, not the top.
• My plasticine work is put on the table to dry a little before it is moved into my bag.

**Words for my notebook**

I roll, flatten, pinch and coil. Big part first, then small parts. Red and yellow make orange.

**Let us talk**

1. Show me how you make a coil.
2. What two colours did you mix, and what did you get?
3. Name the parts of the figure you built.
4. What did you put in your scene?


![A plasticine figure with a coil, a pancake and balls of plasticine.](assets/img/nursery-2/1st-term/cultural-and-creative-arts/week9-plasticine-figure.png)
