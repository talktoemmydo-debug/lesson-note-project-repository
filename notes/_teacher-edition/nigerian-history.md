# Nigerian History — Nursery 2 · First Term

**Weeks:** 1–6, 8–9 (Week 7 Mid-Term, 10 Revision, 11 Examination — no notes).
**Depth floor:** NERDC 2025 · **Primary 1** · Nigerian History · First Term, pp 649–650 — the school
teaches this band **two classes earlier** than NERDC, so each note keeps the Primary 1 content list but
is pitched at a 4–5 year-old: story, picture, talk — no definitions to copy.

---

### WEEK 1 — MEANING AND IMPORTANCE OF HISTORY (PART 1)

**Reference:** NERDC PRIMARY 1 · Nigerian History · First Term W1 (pp 649–650)
**Objectives.** (1) Say in one line what history is; (2) give an example of "the past" from their own
life (yesterday, last week, last year); (3) name two people who tell us about the past; (4) state one
reason we learn history.
**Previous knowledge.** Pupils retell what happened at home yesterday; they have heard bedtime stories.
**Materials.** Three picture cards (baby → child → pupil), a family photograph, an old object from home
(kettle, lamp), the class register.

**Step 1 — What is history?** "History is the **story of the past** — of what happened before now."
Teacher says three sentences and the class puts a hand up for the ones that are past: "I ate this
morning", "I am eating now", "Last year I was smaller".
**Step 2 — Yesterday, last week, last year.** Each child says one thing they did **yesterday**; then one
thing that happened **last week** (a birthday, a funeral, a market day); then **last year** ("I was in
Nursery 1"). The baby picture and today's pupil make the same idea visible.
**Step 3 — Who tells us the past.** Grandma and grandpa, the father and mother, the pastor and imam,
the old men of the street, and **books and pictures** in school. Teacher shows the family photograph and
the old kettle: "These can speak if somebody tells us about them."
**Step 4 — Why we learn it.** "History helps us know about our **families** — who our grandfather was,
where we come from, what people did before us." Close with the sentence of the week: *What happened
before now is still worth knowing.*

**Board summary.** *History is the story of the past. Yesterday, last week, last year are all past. Old
people, photographs and books tell us the past. History tells us about our family.*

**Evaluation.** (1) What is history? (2) Say one thing you did yesterday. (3) Mention two people who
tell you stories of the past. (4) What can a photograph tell us? (5) Say one reason we learn history.
**Assignment.** Ask at home for one story about when you were a baby; come and tell the class tomorrow.

**Worksheet — Week 1 (exam style).**
1. History is the story of A) the future B) the past C) today's lunch D) the sky
2. Which one is in the past? A) now B) this minute C) yesterday D) today
3. Who can tell us about the past? A) a chair B) a plate C) a stone D) a grandparent
4. A photograph of your baby days tells you about A) the past B) next term C) the weather D) the market price
5. Fill in: Last year I was in Nursery ____.
6. Mention two things that help us know about the past.

---

**Teacher's key.** 1-B · 2-C · 3-D · 4-A · 5 one year (Nursery 1) · 6 old people/grandparents, photographs, old objects, books.
### WEEK 2 — MEANING AND IMPORTANCE OF HISTORY (PART 2)

**Reference:** NERDC PRIMARY 1 · Nigerian History · First Term W2 (pp 649–650)
**Objectives.** (1) Name the people in their family tree up to grandparents; (2) say where their family
comes from; (3) state one lesson the past teaches; (4) draw a simple family tree.
**Previous knowledge.** History as the story of the past (Week 1).
**Materials.** Manilla paper, crayons, family photographs, a chart with a family tree drawn on it.

**Step 1 — My family identity.** "You have a name; your family has a name too. _____ family —
that is who you are." Each child says their family name and the class repeats it once, kindly.
**Step 2 — Where we come from.** "Your father's people come from a town: ____. Your mother's people
come from ____." Teacher marks the places on the map/board. Some children come from far; some from
this very street — both are right.
**Step 3 — What the past teaches us.** "Grandma says the man who worked on his farm ate well; the one
who did not, was hungry." So the past teaches us **to work, to be kind, to tell the truth**. And before
we do a thing we can ask: "What happened last time?" — that is how history helps us **make good
decisions**.
**Step 4 — Drawing the family tree.** On the chart: grandparents at the top (grandpa, grandma), then
father and mother, then the children. Each pupil draws their own tree with five names. The trees go on
the wall; the class walks and looks.

**Board summary.** *I belong to a family. My family comes from ______. The past teaches us what is
right. Grandparents → parents → children.*

**Evaluation.** (1) What is your family name? (2) Where do your grandmother's people come from?
(3) Name one thing the past teaches us. (4) Whom do you put at the top of the family tree?
**Assignment.** Finish your family tree at home and add one thing your family is known for.

**Worksheet — Week 2 (exam style).**
1. At the top of a family tree we write A) the children B) the grandparents C) the babies D) the friends
2. Your father's mother is your A) aunt B) sister C) cousin D) grandma
3. The past can teach us to A) lie B) fight C) work hard D) steal
4. Knowing where your family comes from gives you A) an identity B) a shoe C) a pen D) a snack
5. Fill in: My family comes from ____ (town/village).
6. Draw or name the five people in your family tree.

---

**Teacher's key.** 1-B · 2-D · 3-C · 4-A · 5 own town · 6 grandpa & grandma at the top, then father & mother, then the children.
### WEEK 3 — PEOPLES AND PLACES IN THE COMMUNITY (PART 1)

**Reference:** NERDC PRIMARY 1 · Nigerian History · First Term W3 (pp 649–650)
**Objectives.** (1) Say what a community is; (2) name the people who live near us; (3) name four people
who help us in the community; (4) greet a neighbour politely in role-play.
**Previous knowledge.** Family and the street walked each morning (GK Week 5 of the same term).
**Materials.** Picture cards of community workers, a small bench for "neighbour's house", a basket, a cup.

**Step 1 — What is a community?** "The houses around us, the people we meet every day, the street, the
church or mosque, the shop — all of that together is our **community**." Draw the street on the board
and put the pupils' own houses on it.
**Step 2 — The people around us.** Our **neighbours** and **friends**: the man who sells bread, the
woman with the tailoring machine, the driver, the watchman, the teacher. Each child names a neighbour or
a friend and one thing about them.
**Step 3 — People who help us.** **The nurse** (she treats us), **the policeman** (he keeps us safe),
**the driver** (he carries us), **the trader** (she sells food), **the teacher** (she teaches us), **the
cleaner** (he sweeps the street). Teacher names a need — "somebody is sick!" — pupils raise the right card.
**Step 4 — Living near people.** In a community we say good morning, we do not throw dirt in the gutter
of another person, we share what we have. Role-play: carry a basket to the neighbour, ask for a cup of
sugar, say thank you.

**Board summary.** *A community is the people and places around us: neighbours, friends, shops, church,
mosque. Some people help us: nurse, policeman, driver, trader, teacher.*

**Evaluation.** (1) What is a community? (2) Name two of your neighbours. (3) Who keeps us safe?
(4) Who sells food in the street? (5) Say the polite words you used at the neighbour's house.
**Assignment.** Draw three people who help people in your community.

**Worksheet — Week 3 (exam style).**
1. A community is made up of A) only houses B) only children C) only cars D) people and places around us
2. The person who treats sick people is the A) driver B) nurse C) trader D) watchman
3. The man who keeps our street safe is the A) policeman B) baker C) tailor D) baby
4. The person who sells us food is a A) nurse B) teacher C) trader D) pilot
5. Fill in: The people who live beside us are our ____.
6. Mention three people who help us in the community.

---

**Teacher's key.** 1-D · 2-B · 3-A · 4-C · 5 neighbours · 6 nurse, policeman, driver, trader, teacher, caretaker (any three).
### WEEK 4 — PEOPLES AND PLACES IN THE COMMUNITY (PART 2)

**Reference:** NERDC PRIMARY 1 · Nigerian History · First Term W4 (pp 649–650)
**Objectives.** (1) Name the three major ethnic groups in Nigeria; (2) say one thing each group is known
for (language, food, dress); (3) name another group or group's people in our community; (4) state that
different people are to be respected.
**Previous knowledge.** Peoples of the community (Week 3).
**Materials.** A map of Nigeria, pictures of attire (agbada, adire, Hausa babban riga), food pictures
(garri, tuwo, fufu, amala), three short greeting recordings or teacher's voice, cloth samples.

**Step 1 — The big three.** On the map: **Hausa** (in the north), **Igbo** (in the south-east),
**Yoruba** (in the south-west). Each child says which group their people belong to, or which one they
have heard at home. No one is asked to speak for a group they do not know.
**Step 2 — What each group is known for.** **Hausa**: "Sannu" — tuwo and miyan kuka, the big embroidered
**babban riga**. **Igbo**: "Ndewo" — fufu and soup, a red cap in some places. **Yoruba**: "Ẹ̀káàrọ̀" —
amala and ewédú, **àdìrè** and agbadá. Teacher shows the picture, pupils say the greeting, the food,
the cloth.
**Step 3 — Others in our community.** Nupe, Tiv, Kanuri, Ijaw, Efik, Fulani, Igala — "in our street we
also have ____ people." The point is simple: our community is **not one family**; it is many.
**Step 4 — Respect, and the closing line.** We do not laugh at another person's language or food; we do
not say "our group is the best". Then the sentence for the week, said three times: **"We are all
Nigerians."**

**Board summary.** *Hausa, Igbo and Yoruba are the three big groups. We have other groups too. Different
tongue, different food — same country. We are all Nigerians.*

**Evaluation.** (1) Name the three major ethnic groups. (2) Which greeting belongs to the Hausa?
(3) Mention one food of the Yoruba. (4) Name one other group in Nigeria. (5) Say the line of the week.
**Assignment.** Write the names of the three groups and draw one food any of them eats.

**Worksheet — Week 4 (exam style).**
1. Which of these is a major ethnic group in Nigeria? A) Toyota B) Lagos C) Niger D) Hausa
2. "Sannu" is the greeting of the A) Hausa B) Igbo C) Yoruba D) Tiv
3. Amala and ewédú are food of the A) Hausa B) Yoruba C) Igbo D) Kanuri
4. "Ẹ̀káàrọ̀" is a greeting in A) English B) Hausa C) Yoruba D) Igbo
5. Fill in: Though we are different, we are all ____.
6. Name three ethnic groups in Nigeria and one thing each is known for.

---

**Teacher's key.** 1-D · 2-A · 3-B · 4-C · 5 Nigerians · 6 Hausa — Sannu/tuwo/babban riga; Igbo — Ndewo/fufu; Yoruba — Ẹ̀káàrọ̀/amala/àdìrè.
### WEEK 5 — PLACES IN THE COMMUNITY (PART 1)

**Reference:** NERDC PRIMARY 1 · Nigerian History · First Term W7 (pp 649–650)
**Objectives.** (1) Name places in the community and what each is for; (2) match a need to a place;
(3) say how to behave in each place; (4) draw two places in their community.
**Previous knowledge.** Places in the school (GK Week 3) and the street walk (History Weeks 3–4).
**Materials.** Picture cards: school, hospital, market, church, mosque, police station, fire station,
park; a bag for "shopping"; a first-aid coat for role-play.

**Step 1 — Where we learn and where we buy.** **School** — we learn; **market** — we buy food and
things; **hospital** — the sick are treated. Each pupil says what their mother buys at the market.
**Step 2 — Where we pray.** **Church** and **mosque** (and the shrine for traditional worshippers is
named once, respectfully, if the class raises it). Behaviour: we go in quietly, we do not run about, we
respect the people worshipping whatever our own family's place is.
**Step 3 — Where help is kept.** **Police station** — for thieves, for lost people, for any danger;
**fire station** — for fire; both work day and night. Teacher says a bad thing, pupils raise the right card.
**Step 4 — Where we play.** **Parks and playgrounds** — swings, slides, the field under the big tree.
Rule: we take turns, we do not push the small ones. Game: the class walks the wall chart and puts each
card where it belongs.

**Board summary.** *School, market, hospital, church, mosque, police station, fire station, playground —
these are the places of our community.*

**Evaluation.** (1) Where do we buy food? (2) Where do we go when we are sick? (3) Whom do we call when
there is fire? (4) What do we do in the playground? (5) Name a place where people pray.
**Assignment.** Draw two places in your community and write what each one is used for.

**Worksheet — Week 5 (exam style).**
1. We buy tomatoes and fish at the A) hospital B) mosque C) police station D) market
2. A burnt house needs the A) fire station B) school C) park D) tailor
3. A sick person is taken to the A) playground B) hospital C) market D) bus stop
4. A thief is reported to the A) church B) market C) police station D) park
5. Fill in: We learn our books in the ____.
6. Name four places in your community and what each is for.

---

**Teacher's key.** 1-D · 2-A · 3-B · 4-C · 5 school · 6 any four of: school, market, hospital, church, mosque, police station, fire station, playground.
### WEEK 6 — PLACES IN THE COMMUNITY (PART 2)

**Reference:** NERDC PRIMARY 1 · Nigerian History · First Term W8 (pp 649–650)
**Objectives.** (1) Name traditional places in the community; (2) say who rules in the palace; (3) state
what the town hall is used for; (4) describe one monument or old place they have seen.
**Previous knowledge.** The public places of Week 5.
**Materials.** Pictures of a palace (Oba's/Emir's/Igwe's hall), a town hall meeting, an old bridge or
slave route or church/mosque of historical note in the area; a stool/chair for role-play.

**Step 1 — Traditional places.** Every town has **old places** that are not the hospital or the market:
the **palace of the traditional ruler**, the **community hall** where the town meets, and sometimes a
**monument** — a very old building, a bridge, a square where our grandfathers gathered.
**Step 2 — The palace.** In Yorubaland the **Oba**; in the north the **Emir**; in Igboland the
**Igwe** or the eldest man of the kindred; in other lands their own title. The ruler wears beads and a
crown, sits on a stool, gives the title and settles quarrels in his palace. Pupils greet the "ruler" as
their own culture teaches (no child is made to kneel if it is not their custom — a bow or a raised hand
is fine).
**Step 3 — The community hall.** Meetings for the whole town: who will fix the gutter, whose son got a
scholarship, the day of the festival. The class holds a two-minute meeting for one real question — "shall we
water the school garden on Monday?" — and votes by raising hands.
**Step 4 — Picture study or field trip.** The pictures go on the board and the class studies them: "What
is this? Where is it? Is it old or new?" (If the school can walk to a near old building, this becomes a
short trip; the safety rules of Basic Science apply.) Close: **these places belong to all of us.**

**Board summary.** *The palace, the community hall and the old monuments are the traditional places of
our town. The ruler lives in the palace; the town meets in the hall.*

**Evaluation.** (1) Where does the traditional ruler live? (2) What do people do in the community hall?
(3) Name one old thing near your home. (4) What happens in the palace? (5) Whom does a town monument
belong to?
**Assignment.** Draw the palace or the community hall and write one line about what happens there.

**Worksheet — Week 6 (exam style).**
1. The home of a traditional ruler is called A) the market B) the clinic C) the garage D) the palace
2. The place where the whole town meets is the A) community hall B) toilet C) shop D) playground
3. In the north of Nigeria the traditional ruler is the A) Oba B) Igwe C) Emir D) Deji
4. A monument in our town tells us about A) the past B) tomorrow C) the weather D) new shoes
5. Fill in: Town matters are discussed at the ____.
6. Name the ruler's title among the Yoruba, the Hausa and the Igbo.

---

**Teacher's key.** 1-D · 2-A · 3-C · 4-B · 5 community hall · 6 Oba (Yoruba), Emir (north/Hausa-Fulani), Igwe (Igbo).
### WEEK 8 — LIVING TOGETHER IN PEACE

**Reference:** NERDC PRIMARY 1 · Nigerian History · First Term W9 (pp 649–650)
**Objectives.** (1) Say what tolerance means in one line; (2) name three ways pupils show respect for
people of other groups; (3) describe playing and learning together; (4) say two ways of sharing at home
and at school.
**Previous knowledge.** Different ethnic groups (Week 4); peoples of the community (Week 3).
**Materials.** Two cups and one jug of water, a ball, a shared drawing sheet, picture cards of children
playing, a chart "How we make friends".

**Step 1 — Tolerance, in a child's words.** "Tolerance means: **other people are different from me, and
that is all right.**" They eat other food, wear other cloth, speak other words — I do not laugh.
**Step 2 — Respecting people of other groups.** Four rules on the board: we do not mock a name; we do not
say "your god is wrong"; we do not refuse to sit with somebody; we learn to say hello in another
language. Each rule is practised with a partner from a different background if the class allows.
**Step 3 — Playing and learning together.** One ball, twenty children: the game only works when
everybody gets a turn. Shared drawing on one sheet: the picture is nicer when four hands add to it.
Then the class says the sentence: **"What I want for myself, I also want for you."**
**Step 4 — Sharing and caring.** Share biscuit, share crayons, help the child who fell, speak to the one
who is alone. The last point closes the week: **"We are stronger together"** — a bundle of sticks (or a
rope made of threads) does not break as easily as one stick.

**Board summary.** *Tolerance = letting people be different and respecting them. We play together, learn
together, share and care. We are stronger together.*

**Evaluation.** (1) What is tolerance? (2) Say one way of respecting a person of another group.
(3) What happened when the class drew with one sheet? (4) Name two things you can share.
(5) Say the sentence of the week.
**Assignment.** Write three ways you will show tolerance in the class next week.

**Worksheet — Week 8 (exam style).**
1. Tolerance means A) respecting people who are different B) fighting C) laughing at names D) staying alone
2. When a friend falls down we should A) run away B) laugh C) help him up D) hit him
3. One ball in the class means A) only the big boys play B) everybody takes a turn C) nobody plays D) the teacher keeps it
4. To make friends in class we must A) shout B) take things C) refuse to sit with others D) share and greet
5. Fill in: We are stronger _____.
6. Mention four ways of showing respect to people of other groups.

---

**Teacher's key.** 1-A · 2-C · 3-B · 4-D · 5 together · 6 do not mock names, do not say another's god is wrong, sit with everybody, learn to greet in another tongue.
### WEEK 9 — NATIONAL UNITY AND DIVERSITY

**Reference:** NERDC PRIMARY 1 · Nigerian History · First Term W10 (pp 649–650)
**Objectives.** (1) State that Nigeria is one country with many peoples; (2) name differences that exist
among Nigerians (language, food, dress, culture); (3) describe how diversity is celebrated;
(4) identify the Nigerian flag and say what its colours mean.
**Previous knowledge.** The three groups (Week 4), tolerance (Week 8), the flag's colours (GK Week 6).
**Materials.** The Nigerian flag (or a drawing), a map of Nigeria, drums, a cultural dress item or cloth,
pictures of Durbar/Eyo/New Yam festival, chalk.

**Step 1 — One country, many people.** On the map: "This whole country is **Nigeria**. Inside it are
more than **250 groups of people** — Hausa, Igbo, Yoruba, Tiv, Ijaw, Kanuri, Nupe, Efik and many more."
Teacher writes the number 250+ on the board, and each pupil adds the name of their own group.
**Step 2 — Different languages, one nation.** The class counts languages in the room. Then: "If we all
spoke differently, what do we use to be one? **English** is the language we use together, and we still
keep our own tongue at home." One nation, two levels: the family's language and the country's language.
**Step 3 — Different foods, clothes and cultures — and the festivals.** Teacher names and the class
repeats: garri, tuwo, fufu, eba; agbada, babban riga, George wrapper; the **Durbar** in the north, **Eyo**
in Lagos, the **New Yam** festival in the east. "When there is a festival, everybody is invited."
**Step 4 — The flag, and the song.** The flag: **two green strips and one white strip** — green for the
land, white for **peace**. The class stands, sings the national anthem's first line and waves a small
flag once each. Closing sentence, said by everybody: **"Different people, one Nigeria."**

**Board summary.** *Nigeria is one country with more than 250 peoples. Different tongues, foods, clothes
and festivals. Our flag is green and white: land and peace. Different people, one Nigeria.*

**Evaluation.** (1) How many groups of people are in Nigeria? (2) Name three languages you have heard
today. (3) What do the two colours of the flag mean? (4) Mention one festival you know.
(5) Say the sentence of the week.
**Assignment.** Colour the Nigerian flag correctly (two green strips, one white in the middle).

**Worksheet — Week 9 (exam style).**
1. Nigeria has about how many groups of people? A) three B) ten C) more than 250 D) one
2. The colour in the middle of the Nigerian flag is A) white B) green C) red D) blue
3. The green colour on our flag stands for A) water B) milk C) the sky D) the land
4. The language we use to talk to Nigerians of other groups is A) Yoruba only B) English C) Hausa only D) Arabic
5. Fill in: The festival of the New Yam belongs to the ____ people.
6. Mention three ways Nigerians are different and one thing that makes them one.

**Teacher's key.** 1-C · 2-A · 3-D · 4-B · 5 Igbo · 6 different in language, food, dress and festival; one in the flag, the anthem and the use of English.
